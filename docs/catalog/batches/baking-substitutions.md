## Searches and source pages reviewed

Searches covered the King Arthur Baking guidance for soda/powder swaps, egg replacement by baking function and cake/cookie context, yeast-free bread methods, and sourdough additions; Illinois Extension and University of Maine Cooperative Extension egg-replacement tables/tests; and Utah State University Extension substitution guidance. A professional baking text was checked for the narrow use limitation on baker's ammonia. The source directory below gives the exact pages and locators reviewed.

## Findings

### Chemical leaveners

#### Baking soda → baking powder

- **Context supported:** A pinch substitution in a baked recipe that calls for baking soda; the source discusses cakes, cookies, quick breads, and other baking generally, but does not validate a particular formula.
- **Replacement and basis:** For 1 tsp baking soda, use 3 tsp baking powder (volume-to-volume, 3:1). This is King Arthur Baking's stated amount.
- **Adjustments:** No additional acid, liquid, salt, or timing adjustment is prescribed by the source. Keep the missing adjustments unknown rather than deriving them from the ingredient chemistry.
- **Expected effects:** King Arthur says baking soda supports a more tender/airy crumb and more outward cookie spread, while baking powder supplies height/lightness. It warns that the 3x baking-powder amount can produce a slightly bitter, off-putting taste, and says the swap will not perform exactly like the original.
- **Limitations:** Not a 1:1 equivalence; source does not define which recipe formulations tolerate the swap or quantify texture/rise changes. Do not automatically apply when a formula calls for both leaveners, since the source says soda may neutralize recipe acid while powder supplies additional lift.
- **Evidence state:** Supported candidate, narrow “pinch” use; not a universal or automatic substitution.

#### Baking powder → baking soda + cream of tartar + starch

- **Context supported:** Baking recipes that call for baking powder; source gives a pantry replacement formula, without an individual recipe test.
- **Replacement and basis:** For 1 tsp baking powder, combine 1/4 tsp baking soda + 1/2 tsp cream of tartar + 1/4 tsp cornstarch or tapioca starch (volume measures; complete multi-ingredient replacement).
- **Adjustments:** None beyond combining the stated ingredients are prescribed. No extra liquid or timing adjustment is given.
- **Expected effects:** The source describes this as a homemade baking-powder mix. It does not report a specific crumb, color, spread, flavor, or rise comparison.
- **Limitations:** Use the complete formula; soda alone is explicitly rejected as a direct replacement. The source does not specify compatibility with every leavener blend or recipe.
- **Evidence state:** Supported candidate, source formula.

#### Baking powder → baking soda + acid

- **Context supported:** A pinch replacement for baking powder in baking; not tied to a named recipe.
- **Replacement and basis:** For 1 tsp baking powder, use 1/4 tsp baking soda + 1/2 tsp lemon juice or white vinegar (volume measures; complete multi-ingredient replacement).
- **Adjustments:** King Arthur Baking does not say to reduce another liquid or alter salt, temperature, or timing. That balance is therefore unresolved; do not add this formula to an automatic rule without recipe-specific validation.
- **Expected effects:** The source says the acid reacts with baking soda to mimic baking powder's leavening. No sensory or texture comparison is published for this formula.
- **Limitations:** Acid type and recipe context are limited to the listed options. Do not infer equal behavior from other acids or recipe ingredients.
- **Evidence state:** Supported candidate, with an unresolved liquid-balance gap.

#### Baking powder → baker's ammonia

- **Context supported:** King Arthur Baking presents a 1:1 baking-powder replacement; a professional baking text limits baker's ammonia to small products baked dry, such as cookies.
- **Replacement and basis:** For 1 tsp baking powder, use 1 tsp baker's ammonia (volume-to-volume, 1:1), as stated by King Arthur Baking.
- **Adjustments:** No other adjustment is specified.
- **Expected effects:** King Arthur describes a light, tender crumb similar to baking powder. It notes an ammonia smell during baking that it says dissipates by the time the baked good is done.
- **Limitations:** Do not generalize the broad 1:1 statement to cakes, thick/moist products, or other uses: *Professional Baking* gives the dry, small-product limitation. The evidence packet has not validated which particular cookie thickness/formulations meet that condition. Product identity/formulation and exact dose also remain unreviewed.
- **Evidence state:** Conditional candidate for a reviewed dry, small baked product only; otherwise unavailable pending exact context.

#### Other baking-powder replacements listed by Utah State University Extension

USU Extension gives 1 tsp baking powder as replaceable by (a) 1/4 tsp baking soda + 5/8 tsp cream of tartar; (b) 1/4 tsp soda + 1/2 cup fully soured milk or buttermilk; (c) 1/4 tsp soda + 1/2 Tbsp vinegar or lemon juice with sweet milk to make 1/2 cup; or (d) 1/4 tsp soda + 1/4 to 1/2 cup molasses. The page explains that molasses is acidic enough to react with soda.

These are source-published, volume-based candidates, not merged with the King Arthur formulas. USU does not specify how to rebalance the recipe's other liquid, sugar, or other ingredients for the soured-milk, acid/milk, or molasses options. Those candidates therefore remain **not ready for a reusable substitution rule** until a precise recipe context and adjustments are independently supported. Do not average the two sources' cream-of-tartar formulas.

#### Cream of tartar → lemon juice or vinegar for whipped egg whites

- **Context supported:** Stabilizing whipped egg whites when a recipe uses cream of tartar for that function. This is not a baking-powder formula or a syrup/crystallization substitution.
- **Replacement and basis:** King Arthur Baking says that for a recipe calling for a pinch to 1/8 tsp cream of tartar per egg white, start with 1/8 to 1/4 tsp lemon juice or vinegar per egg white. The source gives a range and an instruction to start small; it does not define one exact ratio.
- **Adjustments:** No changes to other liquids, sugar, leavener, or method are specified. Do not invent them.
- **Expected effects:** Lemon juice or vinegar supplies an acidic stabilizer for the whipped foam. King Arthur cautions that strong flavors may be noticeable in the finished result.
- **Limitations:** Keep the direction and egg-white function explicit. The source does not establish a general replacement for cream of tartar in chemical leavening, candy/syrup crystallization, or every recipe containing it. The range is not a volume-for-volume equivalence.
- **Evidence state:** Supported for this narrow egg-white stabilization use; no automatic or general rule is proposed.

#### Baking soda ↔ baking powder: direct equivalence rejected

Equal-amount exchange is explicitly rejected by King Arthur Baking. Soda needs an acidic partner and reacts promptly; double-acting baking powder contains acid and reacts on wetting and again with heat. The source also describes different effects in baking (soda contributes spread/tenderness; powder contributes height/lightness). No 1:1 rule is supported. Use only one of the separately documented directional formulas above where its context fits.

### Yeast and sourdough boundaries

#### Commercial yeast ↔ baking soda or baking powder: no direct conversion supported

- **Context reviewed:** Yeast-raised bread compared with quick breads and other recipes raised chemically.
- **Finding:** King Arthur Baking distinguishes chemical, physical, and biological leavening methods and presents no yeast-to-soda/powder conversion amount. Utah State Extension defines quick bread as bread leavened with baking powder or baking soda instead of yeast. These sources support choosing a complete, suitable no-yeast quick-bread recipe when yeast is unavailable; they do not support converting a yeast-bread formula by exchanging leavener amounts.
- **Adjustments/effects:** No direct dose, rise time, formula adjustment, flavor, or texture equivalence is supported. Chemical leaveners do not supply the fermentation process described for yeast-raised bread.
- **Evidence state:** `researched_no_supported_substitute` for direct yeast ↔ chemical-leavener exchange within this research scope. A separately designed quick-bread recipe is a different recipe, not a substitution record.

#### Sourdough starter → part of recipe flour + water/liquid (not a leavener swap)

- **Context supported:** Incorporating 100%-hydration starter into a recipe that already has enough flour and water or another suitable liquid; King Arthur specifically discusses yeast breads, quick breads, muffins, biscuits, pancakes, scones, and a cake-pan cake. It says cookie recipes generally lack enough water/liquid for this approach.
- **Replacement and basis:** Starter is treated as equal parts flour and liquid by weight. For 8 oz (227 g) starter, remove 4 oz (113 g) flour and 4 oz (113 g) recipe liquid; this replaces part of the flour/liquid pair by weight, not the recipe's baking powder, baking soda, yeast, egg, oil, butter, honey, or other functional ingredient. King Arthur recommends starting with no more than one-third of the recipe flour replaced in its general guidance.
- **Adjustments:** Reduce both flour and suitable liquid as above. Starter hydration may differ; King Arthur says a thinner starter may require a small liquid adjustment but gives no universal amount. If replacing milk, milk fat/solids are lost. For a yeast-bread recipe, it recommends fed starter to help the rise, but gives no general starter-to-commercial-yeast conversion.
- **Expected effects:** More starter can make the baked good tangier. In the cited yeast-bread example the loaf rose and was described as richer in wheat/milk/butter flavor; these effects are recipe-specific, not guaranteed elsewhere.
- **Limitations:** The amount is weight-based and depends on starter hydration. Do not infer that starter replaces chemical leavener, or that starter mass equals any mass of commercial yeast. Sufficient flour/liquid and recipe context are prerequisites.
- **Evidence state:** Supported as a partial flour/liquid replacement/addition under stated conditions; **no supported universal yeast-dose replacement**.

#### Sourdough starter ↔ chemical leavener: direct replacement rejected

King Arthur says starter can be used in recipes leavened with baking soda or baking powder, but its method replaces some flour and liquid and does not instruct removing the chemical leavener. Treat this as starter incorporation only; it is not evidence that starter replaces the chemical leavener. Conversely, no source reviewed gives a starter-to-soda/powder formula. A direct leavener exchange is unavailable.

### Egg-function replacements

An egg can provide structure, emulsification, binding, and/or lift; the role depends on the recipe. No option below is a general one-for-one functional equivalent. The listed count-based formulas are per one whole egg only where the source says so.

#### Egg → liquid/foam replacements for selected cakes

**Aquafaba (liquid from canned chickpeas)**

- **Context supported:** King Arthur recommends it for breads, meringue, and single-layer/snacking cakes; it identifies it as the only listed replacement that whips up meringue-like.
- **Replacement and basis:** 1/4 cup (57 g) aquafaba per 1 egg (volume/mass-to-count, as published).
- **Adjustments/effects:** No other recipe adjustment is given. It adds no flavor/color according to the source and can whip to a meringue-like foam; the source also says it provides little structure.
- **Limitations:** Do not transfer this to cakes that rely on eggs for most structure or leavening. No allergy, dietary-safety, or product-specific claim is made here.
- **Evidence state:** Supported candidate for selected single-layer/snacking cakes and whipped-foam use, not a universal cake replacement.

**Sparkling water/seltzer**

- **Context supported:** Light/fluffy cakes, cupcakes, muffins, or quick breads where eggs are incorporated with liquids; University of Maine Cooperative Extension also tested it in a two-egg yellow cake.
- **Replacement and basis:** 1/4 cup (57 g) seltzer per egg in King Arthur's guide and 1/4 cup per egg in Maine Extension (volume-to-count).
- **Adjustments/effects:** King Arthur describes the result as potentially hit-or-miss and says it does not blend well when eggs are creamed with butter. In Maine's yellow-cake test, seltzer had the best texture/flavor of the alternatives tested but yielded a pale color. Do not universalize that one cake trial.
- **Limitations:** Not supported for a creamed-butter mixing method by King Arthur; the test evidence covers one yellow-cake formula, not all cake styles.
- **Evidence state:** Supported candidate for a liquid-mixed cake/batter; recipe-method restricted.

**Fruit/vegetable purée (applesauce, banana, pumpkin, or sweet potato)**

- **Context supported:** Moist single-layer/snacking or pound cakes, quick breads, and muffins; Illinois Extension's matrix recommends banana, applesauce, or silken tofu in cake/cupcake contexts. Not recommended for crisp/chewy cookies when that texture is intended.
- **Replacement and basis:** King Arthur gives 1/4 cup (57 g) mashed banana or pumpkin purée, or 1/4 cup (64 g) applesauce, per egg. Illinois Extension gives 1/4 cup per egg for moistening and the cake matrix; University of Maine tested 1/4 cup applesauce per egg in a yellow cake.
- **Adjustments/effects:** No specific liquid/sugar correction is prescribed by those sources. King Arthur says purées add moistness and possible chewiness; banana and pumpkin can add flavor/color; excess can add gumminess. It warns that they can make cookies cakey and cakes/muffins denser. In Maine Extension's tested yellow cake, applesauce affected flavor/texture and was described as best suited to quick breads; no quantified rise/color result is provided.
- **Limitations:** Source amounts are not a license to replace multiple eggs in a delicate cake. Keep identity-specific flavor/color and recipe role in view. No exact outcome is established for high-rise or foam-based cakes.
- **Evidence state:** Supported for selected moist, relatively sturdy cakes/quick breads; reject for a target crisp/chewy cookie or airy cake unless exact recipe evidence exists.

**Silken tofu, blended smooth**

- **Context supported:** Moist, dense single-layer/snacking or pound cakes and hearty muffins; Illinois Extension's cake matrix also lists tofu.
- **Replacement and basis:** 1/4 cup (57 g) smooth silken tofu per egg (volume/mass-to-count, King Arthur).
- **Adjustments/effects:** No additional adjustment is stated. King Arthur says tofu adds moisture and can make bakes dense; its flavor/color is described as neutral.
- **Limitations:** Do not generalize to fluffy or foam-raised cakes. Use silken tofu blended smooth as specified; other tofu forms are not supported by this entry.
- **Evidence state:** Supported candidate for dense/moist cake contexts only.

#### Egg → binder replacements for cookies and other low-rise bakes

**Flax or chia gel**

- **Context supported:** Soft/low-rising cookies, brownies, bars, quick breads, and selected yeast baking; Illinois Extension's common-bake matrix recommends flax/water for cookies. King Arthur specifically describes soft, not crunchy, cookies for its detailed flax replacer.
- **Replacement and basis:** Combine 1 Tbsp flax meal or chia seeds with 3 Tbsp (42 g) water per egg; King Arthur's 2021 guide says rest 5–10 minutes until gelatinous. The 2015 King Arthur flax-only guide uses the same 1 Tbsp + 3 Tbsp formula but waits about 30 minutes for the gel to thicken. Keep the preparation time source-specific rather than averaging it.
- **Adjustments/effects:** Helps bind/add body, but does not build structure or aerate well. Flax may create visible flecks, darker color, or a stronger whole-grain flavor. Not recommended for light/fluffy cakes; enriched brioche is excluded from King Arthur's yeast-baking suggestion.
- **Limitations:** Do not claim identical structure or lift to egg. The strongest cookie support is soft/low-rise, not crisp cookies.
- **Evidence state:** Supported candidate for soft/low-rise cookies where binding is needed.

**Starch + water slurry**

- **Context supported:** Cookies, single-layer/snacking cakes, or quick breads; King Arthur recommends it more readily for an already moist cake such as chocolate zucchini cake.
- **Replacement and basis:** Mix 2 Tbsp (14 g) cornstarch, arrowroot, potato starch, or tapioca starch with 3 Tbsp (42 g) water per egg (volume/mass-to-count).
- **Adjustments/effects:** No other adjustment is given. The source says it binds, is less reliable than its other options, and can leave baked goods dry or tender.
- **Limitations:** Not appropriate as an assumed match for every cookie or cake, especially when egg contributes significant structure or aeration.
- **Evidence state:** Lower-confidence candidate, limited to source-listed moist/simple applications.

**Egg-replacement formulas aimed at cookie leavening**

Illinois Extension lists the following formulas under “substitute for leavening ... such as in cookies.” The article separately states that one egg is roughly 1/4 cup, but does not label each formula line explicitly as a one-egg amount. Preserve the formula quantities below as published; treat their exact count basis as unresolved until reviewed:

1. 1 Tbsp flax or chia seed + 3 Tbsp water + 1/2 tsp baking powder.
2. 1/2 tsp baking powder + 1 Tbsp water + 1 Tbsp vinegar.
3. 1 1/2 Tbsp water + 1 1/2 Tbsp oil + 1 tsp baking powder.

These are complete, multi-ingredient candidates. Do not flatten them to a single replacement ingredient. Illinois Extension does not report cookie-type trials, flavor/texture outcomes, additional balance changes, or whether the formulas fit crisp, chewy, or cakey cookies; those fields remain unknown. The first is distinct from the flax/chia binder alone above because this source adds baking powder when the egg's stated role is leavening. Do not publish any of these as a numeric per-egg conversion until the intended count basis is confirmed.

#### Egg replacement candidates with narrow/brand-specific evidence

- **Commercial replacer:** King Arthur says follow the package directions because formulas differ by brand, and identifies banana bread testing for select products. No generic catalog ratio is supported; preserve manufacturer/product specificity if researched later.
- **Greek yogurt:** King Arthur gives 1/4 cup (57 g) per egg for quick breads, hearty muffins, single-layer/snacking cakes, or pancakes. It adds moisture/tenderness; full-fat yogurt gives a richer, more tender result. It is dairy-based (therefore not suitable when the use requires vegan baking), may weigh down the bake or become gummy in larger amounts, and sweetened/flavored products can change flavor. It is not supported here as a general cookie replacement.
- **Cookie cake-like texture:** Applesauce/banana/pumpkin purees can be considered only when a cakier, moist cookie is acceptable. King Arthur explicitly says fruit purées make cookies cakey; this is a rejection for a requested crisp/chewy cookie outcome, not a claim that no cookie formula can use them.

#### Egg-heavy or foam-structured cakes: no general replacement supported

King Arthur says replacement is difficult in sponge cakes, hot milk cakes, chiffon cakes, and angel food cakes where eggs provide most structure/leavening; if the recipe calls for ribbon-stage eggs or folded stiff whites, it recommends choosing another recipe. This is an explicit exclusion, not an invitation to chain aquafaba or other options into an unsupported conversion. Egg-heavy custard pies and soufflés are also outside this batch's cake/cookie recommendation scope. More replaced eggs produce more noticeable differences, according to the same guide.

## Rejected candidates and reasons

| Candidate | Decision | Evidence-based reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1 baking soda ↔ baking powder | Rejected | King Arthur explicitly says equal amounts cannot be substituted; their acid/reactivity and baking effects differ. |
| Baking soda alone for baking powder | Rejected | Baking soda requires an acidic partner; the supported powder replacement includes cream of tartar or another listed acid. |
| Baking powder as a direct replacement for yeast in a yeast-bread formula | Rejected as a conversion | Sources present chemical-leavened quick bread as a different method/recipe class; no dose or yeast-bread conversion is given. |
| Yeast as a direct replacement for baking powder/soda in a quick-bread formula | Rejected as a conversion | No source gives a reciprocal ratio; yeast is biological fermentation and chemical leaveners are presented as distinct methods. |
| Sourdough starter mass = commercial yeast dose | Rejected | No such ratio was found. Starter inclusion guidance replaces flour and suitable liquid by weight; it does not give a general yeast-dose conversion. |
| Sourdough starter as a replacement for baking soda/powder | Rejected | The reviewed starter guidance retains chemical leaveners and only substitutes part of flour/liquid. |
| Applesauce/banana/pumpkin for any cookie egg | Rejected as universal | King Arthur reports cakier cookies; not a match when crisp or chewy texture is required. Illinois' cookie matrix recommends flax/water, not these purées. |
| Flax/chia for a light, fluffy cake or foam-structured cake | Rejected | King Arthur says these gels do not aerate/build structure well and are not ideal for light, fluffy cakes; delicate foam cakes rely on eggs. |
| Any replacement as a universal allergy-safe or dietary-safe substitute | Not assessed / no claim | This batch evaluates culinary function only. Product labels, cross-contact, formulation, and individual suitability are not established by generic ingredient identity. |
