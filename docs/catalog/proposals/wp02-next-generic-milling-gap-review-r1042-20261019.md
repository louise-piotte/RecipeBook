# WP02 Section A7 R1042: generic barnyard millet bran identity hold

**Reviewed:** 2026-10-19  
**Disposition:** Bounded hold; no shared catalog or ledger change.  
**Snapshot audited:** 2,051 ingredient references, 205 forms, 2,456 research-ledger records, 55 legacy candidates, and 114 bundled recipes / 1,123 ingredient lines.

## Scope and selection

The open WP02 Section A7 parent continues review of other grain meals and milling products ([plan, line 2290](../../RecipeBook-global-ingredient-catalog-plan.txt#L2290)). R1042 reviews the generic candidate **barnyard millet bran**, a crop-qualified milling fraction with direct study use in baked foods. This is a generic ingredient review. No brands, product lines, flavours, SKUs, retailer variants, or commercial rosters were researched.

The primary food study establishes that a material called barnyard millet bran was separated from grain and used in rusk and muffin formulations. It does not identify the grain’s botanical species. A later review attributes that bakery work to *Echinochloa frumentacea*, but this secondary attribution does not repair the missing sample-level identification in the primary report. The wider name “barnyard millet” has multiple species and inconsistent taxonomic scopes in food and crop sources. With no settled species boundary and no safe Québec food name, keep the candidate out of active catalog and ledger data.

The earlier Section F barnyard-grain hold and A6 barnyard-millet-flour hold remain separate decisions; R1042 neither reopens nor resolves them. Preserve their evidence and all active millet and bran owners.

## Identity and source boundary

Nazni and Karuna’s 2016 primary study says whole barnyard millet grain was bought at a local market in Salem district, Tamil Nadu; bran was removed by milling, cleaned, shade-dried, manually ground, and sieved through a 60-mesh BSS sieve. The authors then used raw or chemically treated material in rusk and muffin experiments. This directly supports study-specific human-food use of a barnyard-millet-labelled bran fraction. The paper does not give a botanical name, a seed-source specification, or a consumer milling grade. Its market location alone does not establish a species.

The species question remains material. The ICAR–Indian Institute of Millets Research manual cited in the earlier R276 review distinguishes Indian barnyard millet (*E. frumentacea*) and Japanese barnyard millet (*E. esculenta*) as cultivated food-grain species. Health Canada’s crop-group table lists barnyard millet as *E. frumentacea* in a regulatory residue-classification context. FSSAI’s whole/dehulled millet standard uses a different taxonomic set, listing barnyard millet with *E. crus-galli* and *E. colona*. Those sources establish why the unqualified crop phrase is not a reliable species declaration for the 2016 bran sample; they do not identify that sample or reconcile the taxonomic sets.

Bhatt et al.’s later review describes the 2016 bran rusk/muffin work as *E. frumentacea*-based. That is relevant secondary attribution, but the cited primary paper itself does not state this identity or point to a species-declared lot. R1042 therefore does not infer that all products or study samples named “barnyard millet” are *E. frumentacea*, nor does it narrow the candidate to that species on the strength of a review’s summary.

Keep barnyard millet bran distinct from existing bran fractions for foxtail, pearl, proso, finger, and kodo millet. Do not use a broad “millet bran” identity to absorb them or the unresolved barnyard candidate.

## Bilingual boundary

Retain **barnyard millet bran** only as the English research phrase in this proposal. Do not add it as an active display or alias. No Québec French display is established for the unqualified crop or its bran.

Do not borrow **millet des oiseaux** or **millet d’Italie**. The OQLF entry maps those terms to foxtail millet, *Setaria italica*; the existing source ID **WP03-S175** is owned by the foxtail-millet bran reference and remains there. Do not transfer that source or its naming to barnyard millet.

Do not use **millet japonais** or a derivative such as **son de millet japonais**. Earlier R276 sources associate the French crop phrase with *E. frumentacea* in a Québec cover-crop guide and with *E. esculenta* in FAO French crop information. Neither is a Québec human-food name for this bran, and the term does not resolve which species the study sampled. Do not invent a descriptive French picker name while the species boundary is unsettled.

## Measures and substitution limits

The 2016 paper reports that 1 kg of its whole-grain sample contained 350 g of bran and that its sample was hand-ground and sieved through 60 mesh BSS. These figures describe that study’s sample and preparation, not a general bran recovery, grind specification, kitchen measure, or density. Its formulation table tests 0–30 g bran within a 100 g wheat-flour/bran blend for its rusk and muffin experiments. Those experimental levels and the authors’ sensory results are not a home-baking recommendation, general replacement range, or transferable substitution limit. The study’s chemical pretreatments are research protocols, not general household processing or safety instructions.

No reusable mass or volume measure, density, unit mapping, conversion, cooking yield, standardized particle size, or cross-ingredient replacement rule is supported. The bundled recipes provide no direct demand or substitution test. Add no measurement observation, global or contextual substitution rule, form conversion, package yield, or recipe-line substitution link.
