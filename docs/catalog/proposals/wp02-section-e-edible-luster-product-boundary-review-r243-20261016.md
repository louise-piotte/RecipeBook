# WP02 Section E — edible luster and glitter product-boundary review (R243)

- **Review date:** 2026-10-16.
- **Scope:** Luster, pearl, shimmer, and glitter products sold for cake, confectionery, or beverage decoration.
- **Disposition:** Keep a generic edible-luster/glitter ingredient reference unallocated. Canadian decorating listings mix consumable products with decorative dusts explicitly marked non-food; product names such as luster dust, pearl dust, shimmer dust, and edible glitter do not establish interchangeable composition or food-use status. A Québec listing supports a specific Wilton Pearl Dust powder title and an edible-paint use, while a Canadian supplier separately marks some exact products edible and some non-food. No OQLF culinary term, complete product ingredient label, or bundle demand supports a general identity or synonym set. Add no reference, alias, form, legacy mapping, ledger record, recipe link, measure, conversion, yield, or substitution.

## Product boundary

These listings show why “edible” must remain attached to an exact product claim rather than inferred from a decoration name:

- La Guilde Culinaire’s Québec French listing is titled **Colorant alimentaire en poudre Pearl Dust, Or – Wilton**. It describes a gold pearl powder for fondant/sugar-paste decoration and says it can be mixed with Wilton imitation vanilla to make edible paint. The page does not provide a full ingredient declaration or a standalone statement that the powder itself is edible. This supports the exact product wording and a described use, not a generic French catalog term or universal edible status.
- McCall’s Canadian listing **Silver Luster Dust Shimmer 3 g (Edible)** calls the product edible and says it meets food regulations. Its product page gives application instructions and a package size, but no complete ingredient declaration or independently verifiable regulatory basis. Keep those claims SKU-specific; do not infer that other “luster dust” products share them.
- Vanilla Food Company’s Canadian decorating-dust page says its dusts are classified as non-toxic but are not food products and are intended for decoration only. It separates Designer Luster, Royal Pearl, Disco, Blossom, Hybrid Sparkle, FONDUST, and Spirdust product families; it makes separate approval claims for certain named families. This seller distinction demonstrates that “non-toxic,” luster, pearl, shimmer, glitter, and food-grade colorant are not universal synonyms. The seller’s approvals are not a substitute for a reviewed Canadian product label or applicable permitted-use evidence.

The OQLF search found no culinary terminology entry for edible luster dust or edible glitter. The Québec product title above supplies a product-specific French description, not a general term recommendation. Do not add translated variants such as `poudre de lustre comestible`, `poudre nacrée`, `poudre scintillante`, or `paillettes comestibles` as catalog aliases without a matching Québec product or recipe context.

## Recommendation

Leave the edible decorative-dust family unresolved at the catalog level. Do not treat “non-toxic” as evidence of food use, or combine luster dust, pearl dust, shimmer powder, disco dust, glitter, metallic colorants, cocktail shimmer, or edible gold leaf into one identity. A future review can consider a narrow item only when an exact recipe or import request and a current Canadian product label establish the product name, food-use status, ingredient declaration, permitted-use basis, and Québec French wording. Preserve the broader food-coloring references as a separate family.
