# WP02 A7 barley meal: current Canadian product and Québec display review — 2026-10-11

## Disposition

**Hold `candidate-barley-meal` as unresolved. Do not add a catalog reference, alias, form, recipe link, or behavior data.** Current Canadian evidence confirms human-food use of the English phrase, but it does not delimit one stable product class: one Canadian mill calls a ground whole-barley product “meal” across a fine-to-coarse range, while another current Canadian mill calls cross-cut whole-barley pieces “barley meal or grits.” A Canadian retailer's French listing renders one finished-food ingredient as `farine d'orge`, which is already the OQLF-supported display for barley flour. No reviewed source establishes a precise Québec French name for a standalone dry barley-meal product.

## Product boundary and Québec French evidence

The Ontario Made listing for Ottawa Valley Grain Products names a human-food product **Whole Grain Barley Meal** and describes it as crushed and ground from whole barley, with texture spanning fine to coarse. That gives a real product-level identity signal and, at the supplier's category level, the maker lists whole-grain barley meal separately from whole-grain barley flour. It does not provide a particle-size specification, a lower/upper grind boundary, or a French product name. The profile's sentence claiming the barley item includes “wheat bran” conflicts with its barley description and is treated as a likely page error, not evidence about barley composition. The company product-page URL linked from the profile currently returns 404; the profile itself remains readable.

The contrary usage is material. Hinterland Flour Mill's current Yukon product page sells **Steel Cut Barley** and explicitly calls the cross-cut, tiny pieces of whole hulless barley berries “barley meal or grits.” Its shop page showed a listing with a checkout interface when reviewed; the mill's about page also says it is seeking an entrepreneur to take over the operation, so this review does not claim stable retail availability. This product does not establish that all barley meal is steel-cut, but it prevents treating “barley meal” as a consistently finer, flour-like class.

Statistics Canada's NAPCS Canada 2022 all-examples page includes **barley meal for human consumption** under grain products for human consumption, n.e.c. It lists other barley products such as kibbled, hulled, pearled, pot-and-pearl, rolled, and sliced barley, while the wider class also contains other grains' grits, groats, and meals. The classification establishes a recognized human-food category, not a consumer definition, milling specification, or Québec display. The paired French class page does not list barley meal among its illustrative examples; it does not supply a bilingual barley-meal pair. Its separate animal-feed class does list `semoule d'orge pour consommation animale`, which is not transferable to human food.

Health Canada's 2012 barley-product health-claim assessment lists dehulled or hulless barley, pearl barley, flakes, grits, meal, flour, and bran as barley grain-product types. It supports keeping meal, flour, grits, and grain forms conceptually distinct, but does not define a consumer milling boundary or a French display. No eligibility threshold, nutrition claim, serving amount, or other numeric behavior is imported here.

Québec French evidence does not close the gap:

- The OQLF GDT's 1982 `farine d'orge` entry explicitly pairs that preferred term with **barley flour**. This is the formal Québec terminology evidence in this review. It is not a barley-meal definition.
- Metro's current bilingual retailer entry for one Canadian cracker shows **barley meal** in the English ingredient list and **farine d'orge** in its French-language search result for the same SKU. That is a real local rendering, but it is an ingredient declaration in a mixed-grain finished food, not a standalone barley-meal package. The French route redirects to the English page on direct access, so the French text was verified from the indexed listing, not the opened page or a package image. Because it collides with the direct OQLF flour pair, it is not sufficient to register the same term for meal.
- A separate Metro French listing uses **orge finement concassée** in the ingredient declaration for a whole-grain bread topping. It is evidence of local wording for a cracked-barley ingredient, not an equivalent translation of `barley meal`.
- OQLF's food-domain `gruau` entry (2002) defines a cooked oat-flake dish and notes extension to dishes made from other cereals. The Québec recipe site `Recettes du Québec` uses `Gruau à l'orge` for a prepared barley dish. OQLF also has a separate 1983 technical `gruau / grits` entry in the domain of malt crushing. These sources make `gruau d'orge` especially unsafe as a dry product label; neither is a formal term record for dry barley meal.
- The World Intellectual Property Organization's 2026 Nice Class 30 list pairs `barley meal` with `farine d'orge`. It is a trademark-goods classification translation, not a Québec consumer label or OQLF ruling. Its wording demonstrates the same cross-language collision with OQLF's `barley flour / farine d'orge` pair; it cannot resolve the product boundary.

Taken together, the sources support the existence of barley meal as a human-food phrase, but not one unique product scope and not a Québec French display that remains distinct from flour, meal/grits, cracked grain, and prepared barley porridge. The English-only phrase should remain a candidate, not become an active identity or alias.

## Recommendation

Keep `candidate-barley-meal` unresolved. Reopen only when a Québec-market bilingual standalone product or authoritative Québec terminology source identifies a specific dry barley-meal product and provides a French display that can be distinguished from barley flour, whole barley flour, cracked barley, grits/groats, semolina, and a prepared barley dish. Until then, do not add `farine d'orge`, `farine d'orge entière`, `gruau d'orge`, `semoule d'orge`, or `orge concassée` as names or aliases for meal. No numeric, conversion, substitution, or preparation behavior is proposed.
