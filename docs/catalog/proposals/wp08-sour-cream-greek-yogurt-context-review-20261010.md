## Recommendation

Keep ingredient-ref-sour-cream and ingredient-ref-greek-yogurt as distinct ingredients. Keep plain Greek-style yogurt as the existing plain product form under the Greek-style yogurt identity. Keep active plain yogurt separate.

Record these as research-only, direction-specific findings:

1. **Sour cream → plain Greek-style yogurt in selected baked goods:** supported for narrow source-defined contexts. Strong exact-recipe evidence exists for the current Preppy Kitchen Coffee Cake source and the current Sally’s Baking Strawberry Cake source. Both specify a rich/full-fat product condition or a source-specific product option, and the coffee-cake author reports a slightly lighter texture with Greek yogurt. General baking guidance also supports some cakes, quick breads, biscuits, scones, and doughnuts. Do not treat the evidence as a universal cake rule.
2. **Either ingredient as an option in a particular cold dressing:** Utah State University Extension’s ranch dressing formula lists one cup of reduced-fat sour cream or plain Greek yogurt in the same recipe slot, with 1/3 cup buttermilk, 1/3 cup reduced-fat mayonnaise, and one tablespoon of seasoning mix. This supports that recipe’s optional formulation, not every dip or dressing.
3. **Greek-style yogurt → sour cream in uncooked dips, dressings, and baked-potato toppings:** a culinary editor gives general same-amount guidance in both directions for these cold uses and identifies whole-milk Greek yogurt as the closest yogurt option. Treat this as lower-confidence editorial guidance, separate from the source-specific recipe alternatives above. Do not infer a mass ratio or apply it to heated sauces.

Do not activate a runtime rule. The evidence does not supply a consistent amount basis for a reusable ratio, and the current model cannot constrain target yogurt form/fat content or exact recipe formulation. No source reviewed supports sour cream in the bundled crab-ball binder, the specific sriracha dip, tzatziki, or the bundled butter-chicken sauce. Do not infer the reverse direction in baking.

## Direction and context findings

### Sour cream → plain Greek-style yogurt: selected baked goods

- **Exact Coffee Cake source:** Preppy Kitchen’s current Coffee Cake page says sour cream gives a richer, denser cake and permits full-fat Greek yogurt when needed, with a slightly lighter texture. It recommends a yogurt in the 3.5–5% fat range and prefers 5%; it rejects fat-free yogurt for that recipe. The bundled row is 160 g sour cream, but the substitute sentence gives no amount or mass/volume basis. Keep the candidate tied to this coffee-cake formula and preserve the texture difference.
- **Exact Strawberry Cake source page:** Sally’s current ingredient list explicitly presents full-fat sour cream or plain Greek yogurt as alternatives in the same 1/3 cup (80 g), room-temperature line. This source-page option is stronger and more specific than the older bundled row, whose text is 75 g sour cream or plain yogurt. The source page supports the current page’s one-line option; it does not authorize silently changing the bundled recipe.
- **Selected baking beyond those two formulas:** Food Network Kitchen gives equal-parts guidance for sour cream to plain Greek yogurt in scones, quick breads, cakes, and doughnuts, and notes that Greek yogurt generally has less fat and more protein than full-fat sour cream. The page does not define “equal parts” as a mass or volume relation. King Arthur Baking says plain, full-fat Greek yogurt can be used in place of sour cream in biscuits, but gives no replacement quantity; its biscuit discussion warns that liquid choices vary in water, fat, milk solids, and acidity and can affect color and texture. Preppy Kitchen’s separate cake guidance also names Greek yogurt for its Devil’s Food cake family and requires at least 2% fat, preferring 5%; it is not a test of the bundled Devil’s Food formula.
- **Recipe-style extension examples:** Utah State University Extension lists 1/4 cup sour cream or plain Greek yogurt in one banana-bread formula. This supports an option in that named quick bread, not an amount conversion for the RecipeBook cake rows.

**Disposition:** retain a source-supported candidate for the direction sour cream → plain Greek-style yogurt in selected baked goods, with full-fat requirements and source-specific texture results attached to the sources that state them. Do not infer Greek-yogurt → sour cream from these baking findings. Do not copy “equal parts” into a mass ratio.

### Cold dips, dressings, and toppings

- The Kitchn’s senior recipe editor gives same-amount guidance in either direction for uncooked dips, salad dressings, and baked-potato toppings. The article says whole-milk Greek yogurt is the closest yogurt in texture and flavor and notes that yogurt tastes tangier. It is general editorial advice, not a controlled test of every recipe.
- Utah State University Extension’s Homemade Ranch Dressing formula names the complete choice: one tablespoon seasoning mix, 1/3 cup buttermilk, 1/3 cup reduced-fat mayonnaise, and 1 cup reduced-fat sour cream or plain Greek yogurt. It supports either listed choice in that formula. It does not describe sensory differences or a general ratio for other dressings.
- Utah State University Extension also describes Greek yogurt as a sour-cream replacement for dipping quesadillas, but provides no amount. This is support for a cold dipping role only.

**Disposition:** allow a research-only, source-bounded cold-use finding. The sources do not support applying a generic cold-dip rule to the bundled Sriracha mayonnaise sauce, tzatziki, or unrelated dressing formulas. The two cold Crab Balls yogurt rows do not have a source-tested sour-cream option.

### Soups and heated sauces

Christopher Kimball’s Milk Street describes Greek yogurt replacing sour cream in soup only with method limits: for soup without starch, stir it in at the end and do not boil; the source says boiling can curdle either yogurt or sour cream. If the soup must boil, the article advises that it contain starch such as flour or potatoes. For baking, it recommends whole-fat Greek yogurt, especially when the recipe uses more than 1/4 cup sour cream.

**Disposition:** this is a distinct, directional, method-sensitive soup finding. It is not evidence for Greek yogurt → sour cream, for arbitrary sauces, or for the 1/2 cup yogurt in Butter Chicken, whose recipe boils and simmers the yogurt-containing sauce. Do not import an untested adjustment into that recipe.
