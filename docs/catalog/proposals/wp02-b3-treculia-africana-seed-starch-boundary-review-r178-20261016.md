# WP02 B3 R178 — *Treculia africana* seed-starch boundary review

- **Reviewed:** 2026-10-16.
- **Status:** Proposal only. This file is the sole change; no shared JSON, ledger, plan, roadmap, batch record, code, or generated file was edited.
- **Plan fit:** One new regional starch candidate under the still-open WP02 B3 checklist item, “Water-chestnut flour and other documented regional starches.” This review concerns laboratory-isolated starch from *Treculia africana* (African breadfruit/ukwa) seeds, not flour made from the edible whole seed.
- **Proposal-local source IDs:** `WP02-B3-TRECULIA-SEED-STARCH-R178-20261016-S01` through `S05`. These are provenance labels only and are not shared-ledger records.

## Recommendation

**Hold a separate *Treculia africana* seed-starch identity and all aliases.** A primary starch-science paper isolates starch from dehulled *Treculia africana* seed flour with aqueous sodium hydroxide, reports lab-scale characterization, and concludes that the starch would require modification to improve its use in processed foods. Current Canadian-facing evidence found whole Ukwa seeds for cooking, and current Québec-facing evidence did not establish a standalone starch product or French product name. The catalog already distinguishes seedless *Artocarpus altilis* breadfruit starch from breadnut and jackfruit seed-starch research; a new generic `African breadfruit starch` display would further blur botanically distinct foods. The evidence establishes an experimental starch fraction, not a retail culinary starch class. [S01–S05]

Keep `African breadfruit starch`, `ukwa starch`, `Treculia starch`, and descriptive French compounds as research-only terms. Reopen if a current Canadian or Québec food product or authoritative food specification identifies isolated *Treculia africana* seed starch for culinary sale and supplies product-matched French wording. Do not map Ukwa flour, whole Ukwa seeds, *Artocarpus* breadfruit starch, or breadnut/jackfruit seed starch to this candidate.

## Product and naming boundary

*Treculia africana* is called African breadfruit in English and Ukwa (Igbo), Afon (Yoruba), and Ize (Edo), but the consumed Canadian product located is whole dried seed sold for boiling, roasting, or cooking. Separate food research uses flour made from whole seeds in formulated snack bars. Neither establishes isolated seed starch as the same product or supports aliasing the whole-seed names to starch.

The 2011 starch study's material is not simply milled Ukwa flour: seed flour was dispersed in dilute aqueous sodium hydroxide, sieved, allowed to settle, and the separated starch was washed and dried. The method and experimental starch properties are research evidence only. No French term for *Treculia*-seed starch was found in OQLF or current Canadian/Québec-facing product material. Do not adopt `amidon de fruit à pain africain` or `amidon de graines d’ukwa` as a display or alias without product-name support.

## Numeric and substitution boundaries

The seed-starch study reports a laboratory starch recovery of 36.0–41.7% from its prepared seed flour, gelatinization and paste properties, and functional test results. These measurements are tied to isolated lab samples and a chemical extraction method; they are not a home-preparation yield, commercial-product composition standard, bulk density, or kitchen measure. The separate Ukwa-flour snack-bar study uses processing treatments and blend levels for flour, not isolated starch. Do not transfer its flour ratios to starch or treat them as a general substitution. No forms, measures, density, unit mapping, conversion, yield, recipe links, or active/global/contextual substitution rules are proposed.

## Reopen criteria

Reconsider this identity only after evidence establishes a human-food isolated-starch product that is sold or specified for culinary use in Canada or Québec, with a source-matched French name. Keep any future identity taxon- and plant-part-qualified, distinct from *Artocarpus altilis* fruit starch and *Artocarpus* seed-starch research. Any measurement or substitution must be researched separately against the actual consumer product and remains unknown until then.
