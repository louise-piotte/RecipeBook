# WP03 Section F — Ethiopian Beso/Besso product-boundary hold (2026-10-13, R66)

- **Status:** proposal only; no catalog, ledger, roadmap, or checklist data changed.
- **Scope:** one Ethiopian regional preprocessed-grain candidate with the English spellings Beso and Besso (Amharic በሶ), also encountered as “beso/besso flour.”
- **Recommendation:** keep Beso/Besso unallocated for now. Do not add either spelling or a qualified flour phrase as an alias on the existing roasted-barley-flour reference, and do not create a separate picker row or ledger candidate in this proposal.
- **Reason:** Ethiopian field sources document barley-based roasted flour and its wet preparations, while a CIMMYT preparation manual describes Besso as medium-roast cereal flour, commonly barley, and gives a maize-based QPM version. Retail evidence now includes both a single-ingredient Yegna Enat product labeled “Roasted Barley Flour (Beso)” and a Canada-shipped product combining roasted barley flour, cinnamon, and peanuts. This confirms that one commercial Beso product is plain dry barley flour, but the reviewed sources do not settle whether a bare Beso/Besso search consistently means that product, another roasted-cereal flour, a formulated dry mix, or a prepared food. No Beso/Besso-specific Québec French product name was located. The existing Québec roasted-barley-flour row therefore cannot safely own the bare term.

## Evidence and product boundary

The strongest process description is a field study based on interviews with 275 households in 11 districts across three zones of Ethiopia’s Amhara Region. It reports that Beso flour is made from roasted barley; the grains may or may not be dehulled, and a common process roasts the grain, partially dehulls it, coarsely mills and sieves it, then finely mills and sieves it again. In the described preparation, water is added to moisten the flour so it can be rolled or balled by hand. Salt is usual in the water, while sugar or spiced butter may be added. The same flour is used for shamia, a drink. The authors explicitly describe their field data as descriptive rather than quantitative [S01].

This source establishes a real regional roasted-barley flour, while also showing that a bare name can cross ingredient and prepared-food states. A separate 2022 barley-use study distinguishes Beso from neighboring products: Kolo is roasted whole grain, Chuko combines roasted barley flour (Beso) with spiced butter, Kinche is cracked grain, and Genfo is a flour porridge. Its table lists foods by district, so it does not establish one national retail specification [S02].

The CIMMYT Quality Protein Maize preparation manual uses the double-s spelling Besso. It describes Besso as a food made from medium-roast cereal flours, commonly barley, and supplies a maize-based QPM version. Its wet preparation specifies 200 g QPM Besso flour with 50 ml water plus seasoning/oil; its drink uses 200 g QPM Besso flour, 200 ml water, and 20 g sugar. Those are scoped QPM recipes, not a barley-flour specification or a reusable conversion. The manual demonstrates that the name can travel across cereal bases [S03].

A current Canada-facing seller uses the product title “Beso (በሶ),” gives a 500 g package size, and ships across Canada. Its ingredient statement is “Roasted Barley Flour (Beso), Cinnamon, Peanuts”; the seller describes mixing the product with water or milk. This verifies Canadian availability of one formulated Beso product, not a universal Beso recipe or a pure-flour product standard [S04]. The package size is not a household measure.

A second current retail listing is a useful counterexample to treating every commercial Beso as a blend. Afrikart lists a Yegna Enat product titled “Roasted Barley Flour (Beso).” Its product details call it roasted grain flour/powder, say the barley is roasted and finely milled, identify roasted barley as the main ingredient, and list “100% Roasted barley flour” as the ingredient. The listing says the product is crafted in Ethiopia and ships from a U.S. warehouse; it gives a 1 kg package weight [S09]. This is direct seller evidence for one plain, dry barley-flour product called Beso. It does not establish that every bare Beso/Besso label has that scope, that the item is sold in Québec, or that its process is equivalent in every detail to the active Québec roasted-barley-flour identity. The 1 kg package weight is not a kitchen measure. The page text was available in the web search index during this review, while a direct page open returned a cache miss.

EPHI’s official research page lists its Ethiopian Food Composition Table 2025 and Ethiopian Foods Recipe Book 2025, and FAO hosts a record for the 2025 table. The linked EPHI PDFs returned 403 during this review. I did not use search-indexed copies or scraped table text to establish a product definition, recipe, or numeric value [S05].

## Forms, measures, conversions, and substitutions

- The existing roasted-barley-flour row has no forms, density, or unit mappings; its ledger record is identity-verified but has measurementState not_yet_researched and substitutionResearchState not_yet_researched, with no measurement observations or substitution findings.
- The Ethiopian field study describes process and preparation variation, but reports descriptive field information rather than quantitative ingredient measurements [S01]. It does not supply a reusable flour mass-per-volume, standardized hydration ratio, yield, or substitution amount.
- The 2017 CIMMYT recipes give water amounts for maize-based QPM Besso flour preparations, not barley-flour behavior [S03]. Do not transfer their ratios or any implied product replacement to the active roasted-barley-flour row.
- The 2008 Ethiopian Ministry of Health clinical-nutrition manual gives source-specific prepared serving examples: Beso drink is 55 g for Beso plus sugar, and Beso firfir is 65 g for Beso plus oil. The amounts include other ingredients and are prepared-snack portions, not the mass of plain dry flour in a standard spoon or cup. The manual’s tablespoon definition is specific to its context [S06]. Do not turn these examples into a catalog density or unit mapping.
- The current 500 g Canadian package is net package size only [S04]. It does not establish serving size, conversion, or density.
- The Afrikart listing gives a 1 kg package and describes a plain roasted-barley-flour product [S09]. It supplies no volume measure, flour density, standardized preparation ratio, yield, or substitution evidence; package net weight is not a kitchen conversion.
- No source reviewed here tests or scopes a substitution between Beso and the active roasted-barley flour, barley flour, malted barley flour, bsissa, another cereal flour, or a prepared Beso form. Add no form, measure, density, conversion, yield, recipe link, or substitution rule.

## Integration sketch and disposition

Keep the active references and the research ledger unchanged. Keep the existing generic roasted-barley-flour identity and its measurement/substitution review states unchanged. Do not add Beso, Besso, or either qualified flour spelling to any alias array; do not add a standalone identity or recipe link from this proposal. The Afrikart listing changes the evidence summary by establishing one current single-ingredient product labeled as Beso, but the hold disposition remains because that seller-specific scope does not resolve general term ownership, process/product variation, or the missing Québec French name.

If a later recipe or a Québec-facing package establishes a specific product, first distinguish plain dry roasted barley flour from other roasted-cereal flour bases, seasoned/flour blends, and moistened or shaped preparations. Reuse the roasted-barley-flour identity only if a source establishes the plain barley-flour scope as equivalent; otherwise define a separate product only when its formulation and bilingual naming are stable. Any later measure or substitution requires exact-form evidence rather than QPM recipe ratios, the package weight, or clinical prepared-serving examples above.
