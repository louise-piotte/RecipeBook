# WP06 — Mexican dried chile boundaries

- **Reviewed:** 2026-10-09
- **Status:** Source-grounded proposal only; no shared catalog, ledger, recipe, test, roadmap, plan, or generated-file edits.
- **Scope:** Ancho, guajillo, pasilla, mulato, cascabel, chile de árbol, chipotle, and morita; their fresh counterparts, dry and smoke states, aliases, and safe EN/fr-CA display wording.
- **New source-ID prefix:** WP06-MEX-DRIED-CHILES-20261009-S. Repository preflight found no existing IDs with this prefix. The SEP source from the earlier Mexico/Central America sweep is reused by its original ID, not registered again.

## Recommendation

Treat the source-named dry chiles as separate, searchable culinary products, while recording a fresh-to-dry relationship only where direct sources support it. These names are not general synonyms for the fresh pepper. Model named dry presentations as distinct forms only when the candidate fresh identity is sufficiently bounded; otherwise defer the form link and keep the dry product distinct.

The clearest form relationships are poblano → ancho, chilaca → pasilla, mirasol → guajillo, fresh/dried chile de árbol, and red jalapeño → chipotle. Keep ancho and mulato separate as two named dried poblano-type products; do not merge them or treat one as the alias or substitute for the other. The current Mexican whole-dried-chile standard recognizes cascabel as its own type, but the proposed fresh counterpart “bola” is not sufficiently bounded for a universal form mapping. Morita is a named dried/smoked jalapeño-related product; do not alias it to chipotle or meco.

This proposal does not recommend a botanical species field for each culinary name, a universal cultivar crosswalk, or a substitution. Standards classify commercial product types and establish scope; they do not settle every regional cultivar, local name, or culinary equivalence.

## Proposed EN and fr-CA wording

Keep the Mexican product name visible in both languages. Qualify state in the display name whenever the recipe needs to distinguish fresh, whole-dried, ground, or smoked product. Only ancho and chipotle have direct OQLF naming evidence among these candidates; other French labels remain provisional.

| Candidate product | Proposed EN display | Proposed fr-CA display | Language evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ancho | dried ancho chile | piment ancho séché | OQLF calls the dried poblano piment ancho. |
| Guajillo | dried guajillo chile | piment guajillo séché | Provisional. A Québec food distributor lists “Piment guajillo”; this supports local market usage, not a terminology ruling. |
| Pasilla | dried pasilla chile | piment pasilla séché | Provisional; preserve the Mexican name. |
| Mulato | dried mulato chile | piment mulato séché | Provisional; do not translate the name as a color-only descriptor. |
| Cascabel | dried cascabel chile | piment cascabel séché | Provisional; preserve the Mexican name and state. |
| Chile de árbol | dried chile de árbol | piment de árbol séché | Provisional; retain the Spanish name, including its accent. Accent-folded search handles the unaccented input. |
| Chipotle | whole dried and smoked chipotle chile | piment chipotle entier séché et fumé | OQLF recognizes chipotle as the name taken by a jalapeño when smoked and dried. The full product wording is a descriptive display, not an OQLF-validated phrase. |
| Morita | dried and smoked morita chile | piment morita séché et fumé | Provisional; preserve morita as the product name. |

Do not use literal “tree chile” as a global English alias for chile de árbol. Do not use generic English “chili,” “chili powder,” or “chili flakes” as aliases for any named whole dried chile.

## Candidate decisions

| Candidate | Decision | Fresh/dry boundary and safe matching |
|---|---|---|
| **Ancho** | **Accept as a named dried form of poblano.** | OQLF explicitly identifies piment ancho as the dried name of piment poblano; SADER and SEP also pair poblano with ancho. If a poblano reference is present or added, attach a separately matchable dried-ancho form. Do not add ancho as an unqualified alias for fresh poblano or poblano as an alias for dried ancho. |
| **Mulato** | **Accept as a separate named dried product; keep distinct from ancho.** | The current standard lists mulato separately from ancho. INIFAP describes mulato as a poblano type with a distinct flavor and warns against treating it as interchangeable with ancho. It is related to the poblano commercial group, but the evidence does not bound one universal fresh cultivar-to-mulato mapping. A later form model may attach a named “dried mulato” form to a suitably scoped poblano identity; do not use a generic poblano alias or collapse it into ancho. |
| **Guajillo** | **Accept as a named dried form of mirasol, with the state qualifier retained.** | SADER and SEP pair fresh mirasol with dried guajillo; the Mexican dried-chile standard names guajillo and an earlier standard gives “guajillo (mirasol).” A state-qualified term such as “dried mirasol chile” is a safe candidate match term for the dried guajillo form. Do not make bare mirasol a global alias: Mexican sources also use mirasol for a fresh chile. |
| **Pasilla** | **Accept as the dried form of chilaca.** | SADER, SEP, and INIFAP directly pair chilaca with pasilla. Store the names as separate fresh and dried form labels, not interchangeable aliases. INIFAP also distinguishes pasilla from pasilla de Oaxaca; defer that latter product and do not alias it to ordinary pasilla. |
| **Cascabel** | **Accept as a distinct dried-chile product; defer a universal bola form mapping.** | The current standard lists cascabel among whole dried chile types, separate from catarina. INIFAP profiles it as a regional dry product and notes that catarina resembles it. SADER pairs bola and cascabel in a general table, but that alone does not establish that every fresh bola is the specific dried cascabel product. Do not alias bola or catarina to cascabel; preserve the SADER pair only as a source-scoped lead. |
| **Chile de árbol** | **Accept one named chile identity with fresh and dried forms.** | SADER and SEP show the fresh/dried relationship, and the current standard includes whole dried de árbol. The name persists across states, so a recipe line that requires the dry product should say “dried chile de árbol.” Do not treat the fresh and dried presentations as the same form or add a literal “tree chile” alias. |
| **Chipotle** | **Accept as a dried-and-smoked form of the active jalapeño-pepper identity.** | The active Mexican standard defines chipotle/chilpotle as red jalapeños, any variety, that are whole, dried or dehydrated, and wood-smoked. OQLF independently records that the jalapeño takes the name chipotle when smoked and dried. Accept chilpotle as a spelling variant of the chipotle form; defer chicpocle, which appears only as an informal spelling in the SIAP article. Keep chipotle in adobo separate as the existing compound condiment. A bare chipotle search will also find that compound product; use a qualified dried/smoked form display, and do not add chipotle as an alias of fresh jalapeño. |
| **Morita** | **Accept as a separately named dried/smoked jalapeño-related form; defer its relationship to generic chipotle and meco.** | SIAP describes chipotle production from jalapeño or its morita variety; INIFAP describes morita as jalapeño-like and used dry and smoked. That supports the jalapeño relation and a separate searchable morita form label, but not a universal alias to chipotle. The earlier SEP infographic maps meco to morita, which conflicts with the jalapeño-linked accounts. Defer the meco crosswalk and do not alias morita, meco, mora, or generic chipotle to one another. |

### Product-state boundaries

The cited standards and agricultural descriptions support whole dried products and, for chipotle and morita, drying plus smoking. They do not support treating ground chile, powder, flakes, paste, puree, seasoning blends, or sauces as the same form as a whole dried chile. Keep those as separate presentations or compound products if later evidence and recipe use justify them. The active generic chili powder and red chili flakes remain separate from every candidate here.

No density, unit mapping, cooking yield, rehydration ratio, conversion, heat ranking, or substitution is proposed. The INIFAP material includes product-specific preparation and quality observations; this review does not generalize those observations into catalog data.

## Evidence limits

- The active Mexican dry-chile standard confirms commercial dry-type names and scope. It does not prove that each name is a cultivar, a stable botanical variety, or a globally uniform recipe product.
- The SADER and SEP fresh/dry tables are useful Mexican naming evidence, not universal botanical crosswalks. The SEP infographic’s meco-to-morita mapping conflicts with SIAP and INIFAP descriptions relating morita to jalapeño.
- Ancho has direct OQLF fresh/dry terminology. No Québec terminology ruling was found for guajillo, pasilla, mulato, cascabel, chile de árbol, or morita; proposed French labels remain provisional.
- The OQLF jalapeno entry spells the French term without the Spanish tilde and records chipotle as the dried/smoked name. Existing catalog spellings were not changed in this proposal.
- The active French jalapeño display currently retains the Spanish tilde; OQLF recommends jalapeno without it in French. This is recorded as a terminology-review point only and does not change the active row.
- This review did not investigate every state, market, cultivar, Indigenous-language name, regional smoked process, or prepared product. The evidence is not enough to globalize bola/cascabel, meco/morita, pasilla de Oaxaca, or bare mirasol aliases.
