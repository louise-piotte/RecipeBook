# WP06 African pantry follow-up — Abacha product state and Québec naming

- **Reviewed:** 2026-10-11
- **Status:** Proposal only. No catalog, ledger, plan, roadmap, recipe, code, or test change is made.
- **Recommendation:** Keep `candidate-abacha` held, but narrow the future candidate to the packaged **dried shredded cassava strips** sold as Abacha. Do not treat wet slices, rehydrated strips, prepared `Abacha Ncha`, tapioca products, or dried cocoyam chips as the same selectable ingredient.
- **Scope:** Follow-up to the held WP02 Abacha review while the broader WP06 African cuisine and Section Z checklists remain open.
- **Evidence accessed:** 2026-10-11.

## Decision

New evidence resolves the state of a specific commercial item more clearly than the prior review did. A current Canadian retailer explicitly lists `Abacha (Dried Shredded Cassava)`, another Canadian retailer describes its Abacha product as dried cassava shredded into thin strips, and the 2022 food-science study calls its locally purchased commercial Abacha strips dried. A standards notice also lists the name `Dried shredded cassava (Abacha)` and the French title `Manioc râpé séché (Abacha)`. These sources support a **narrow dried-strip candidate**, not a general identity spanning every use of `Abacha`.

The Québec naming gap remains. OsiAfrik’s current French-facing Canadian storefront lists only `Abacha 227 g` and says it ships to Canada; it does not locate the seller in Québec or describe the product state. The standards title supplies a useful French descriptor, but it is not Québec usage, and the full specification was not retrieved. The OQLF entry located for `manioc` names the plant/root, not an Abacha product term. Exact-term searches of current Montréal and Québec City African-grocery storefronts did not surface an Abacha product page; that is an indexed-site check, not evidence that no local shop carries it.

Therefore, **hold catalog promotion pending Québec-specific product naming or a coordinator decision to accept a provisional descriptive French label**. If later promoted, limit it to dried shredded cassava sold as Abacha. A defensible provisional display pair would be `Abacha (dried shredded cassava)` / `Abacha (manioc râpé séché)`, with the French descriptor marked provisional. This proposal does not recommend an alias set, category, form, measure, conversion, yield, recipe link, or substitution.

## Evidence by product state

| State or meaning | Evidence and boundary | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Dried shredded cassava strips** | Illoh et al. identify commercial dried Abacha strips and rehydrate them to prepare `Abacha Ncha`. Current Canadian listings from Beauty For Ashes and iLeOja call the retail item dried shredded cassava. The standards notice’s English and French titles name this same dried product state. | Supported as a narrowly scoped retail ingredient candidate. It is the only state recommended for any future row. |
| **Wet cassava slices** | FAO reports that Abacha may occur as wet or dry slices and describes wet slices in snack or bean-dish contexts. | A source-attested separate state; it is not established as the dry packaged retail item reviewed here. Do not fold it into the dried-strip candidate. |
| **Rehydrated cassava strips** | Illoh et al. soak dried strips and drain them before mixing with `ncha`. This is a preparation step for a dish, not evidence of a separately sold ingredient or a stable catalog form. | Keep as a recipe-preparation state only. No generic rehydration amount, ratio, or yield follows from the study protocol. |
| **Prepared `Abacha Ncha` / African salad** | The study distinguishes dried strips from the finished Abacha Ncha made with rehydrated strips and `ncha`. FAO also describes dried slices as an ingredient used to prepare African salad. | Finished dish, not an ingredient alias. Keep `African salad`, `salade africaine`, and `Abacha Ncha` out of a global alias set. |
| **Tapioca** | FAO parenthetically calls Abacha “tapioca” in its Igbo-food discussion, but OQLF and the live catalog distinguish manioc root from tapioca products. The current catalog has separate tapioca-starch and tapioca-pearls references. | Contextual wording does not establish equivalence to tapioca starch or pearls. Do not alias. |
| **Dried cocoyam chips called Abacha** | The same FAO chapter later says cocoyam can be made into dried chips called Abacha and mentions an Abacha dish with pigeon pea and dried cocoyam chips. | A source-specific naming collision. It blocks using bare `Abacha` as a broad global synonym across cassava and cocoyam products. |

FAO’s processing description and the study’s soaking protocol are used only to distinguish states. They do not establish a household instruction for this app, safe processing guidance, a conversion, or a transferable yield. The Ekwu et al. study’s comparison of Abacha slices produced from fresh roots and dried chips likewise shows distinct processing routes, not an ingredient conversion.

This single product-boundary follow-up does not close or narrow those parent checklist items.

Read-only audit of the current worktree found:

- 962 active ingredient references, 53 forms, and 75 legacy-import candidates.
- No active reference, form, or legacy key for `Abacha` or `Abacha Ncha`.
- Existing cassava references cover the root, leaves, edible whole-root flour, tapioca starch/pearls, gari, attiéké, and separate Brazilian starch products. Those are neighboring identities, not Abacha rows.
- All 114 manifest-listed recipes and 1,123 ingredient lines were scanned for `Abacha`, `Abacha Ncha`, cassava shreds/strips, and African salad wording; there were no hits. No recipe demand or quantity is inferred.
- No ingredient form, serving measure, density, unit mapping, conversion, yield, or substitution is supported by this follow-up.

Counts are a worktree snapshot only and should be rechecked before any integration.

## Recommended future boundary

If the coordinator later accepts the dried-strip identity with a provisional French descriptor, limit it to the shelf-stable dried shredded cassava product called Abacha. Keep the bare source term in display text rather than activating broad aliases. Do not add `African salad`, `salade africaine`, `Abacha Ncha`, `tapioca`, generic `cassava`/`manioc`, `cocoyam`, `wet`, or `rehydrated` as aliases or forms. Keep all numeric behavior and recipe links empty until separate, exact-source evidence supports them.

The remaining evidence that would change the hold is a Québec-specific retailer/manufacturer label or a Québec institutional/community source that names this exact **dried shredded cassava** product in French. A Québec source for wet slices or the prepared dish would not close that gap.
