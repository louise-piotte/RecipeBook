## Coordinator recommendation

1. **Do not add one generic `baking ammonia` / `baker’s ammonia` identity.** The name is assigned to ammonium bicarbonate by one baking-industry reference, to ammonium carbonate by another, and to historical mixtures in a peer-reviewed review. “Hartshorn,” “salt of hartshorn,” and “sal volatile” also appear as components or mixtures, not stable one-to-one names [S05, S06, S09–S10, R09–R11]. Keep these names unresolved unless a recipe or package identifies a specific salt or composition.
2. **Recommend two distinct chemically named ingredient identities:** `ammonium bicarbonate / bicarbonate d’ammonium` and `ammonium carbonate / carbonate d’ammonium`. OQLF and Canadian sources give distinct bilingual names, and Canadian and U.S. food references list the ingredients separately [R01–R05]. Candidate category for each: `BAKING_AND_SPICE`, matching the current baking soda and baking powder owners. Do not alias either identity to the other. No recipe links are justified because the live corpus has no matching row.
3. **Treat ammonium carbonate as a food ingredient name, not a purity guarantee.** The current U.S. food standard defines ammonium carbonate as a mixture of ammonium bicarbonate and ammonium carbamate; the 2025 USDA-commissioned technical evaluation says commercially available material is not pure and may have differing composition [R07–R08]. Do not store `(NH4)2CO3` as an invariant product composition or infer one fixed bicarbonate/carbamate ratio. Require product-specific specification evidence if composition matters.
4. **Keep aqueous ammonia separate.** OQLF defines `ammoniaque` / `hydroxyde d’ammonium` as a solution of ammonia in water and notes that it is used in household products; `ammoniac` is gaseous ammonia [R12]. Do not alias `household ammonia`, `ammoniaque`, `ammonia water`, or `ammonium hydroxide` to either dry leavener. Health Canada lists ammonium hydroxide separately for food-additive uses; that does not make household ammonia a food ingredient [R04–R05].
5. **Keep all measures and replacement advice inactive.** King Arthur’s volume-to-volume baking-powder replacement names only generic baker’s ammonia, without specifying which salt. It cannot populate a salt-specific conversion or substitution rule. Preserve it as a source-scoped observation only; do not infer a mass equivalent, density, or universal moisture threshold [existing `WP08-S01`, existing baking-substitutions S1/S11, S06, R09–R10].
6. **No recipe linkage is proposed.** The current 114-recipe corpus contains no ammonium, ammonia, hartshorn, or sal volatile ingredient rows. Keep the plan’s other Section E baking agents and the wider section open.

### Québec French decision

For ammonium bicarbonate, OQLF’s entry is headed **carbonate acide d’ammonium** and lists **carbonate acide d’ammonium**, **hydrogénocarbonate d’ammonium**, and **bicarbonate d’ammonium** as preferred terms. It marks **hydrogénocarbonate d’ammonium** as systematic nomenclature. CFIA’s French permitted-synonym page uses **bicarbonate d’ammonium** as the common name and lists the other two as permitted synonyms [R01, R03]. This packet recommends **bicarbonate d’ammonium** as the recipe display and the other two as aliases; the Québec terminology source itself supports all three.

For ammonium carbonate, OQLF’s GDT entry gives **carbonate d’ammonium** [R02]. The OQLF page was still unavailable to direct retrieval on 2026-10-12, but its official search-indexed entry supplies the preferred term and English equivalent; Health Canada’s French list independently uses **carbonate d’ammonium** as the separate additive name [R04]. Do not translate “baker’s ammonia” literally: no authoritative Québec source reviewed here establishes a specific French consumer name for that ambiguous trade/common term.
