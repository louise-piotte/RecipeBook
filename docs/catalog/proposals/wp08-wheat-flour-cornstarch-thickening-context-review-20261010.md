# WP08 proposal: wheat flour and cornstarch for thickening

**Review date:** 2026-10-10  
**Disposition:** Research-only. Do not add or activate a runtime substitution rule from this review.  
**Scope:** The active all-purpose wheat flour identity versus cornstarch when the ingredient's function is thickening a sauce, gravy, starch-thickened pudding, or starch-thickened pastry cream. This is not an identity, synonym, gluten-free, or allergy-safety claim.

## Recommendation

- Keep the existing **cornstarch → all-purpose flour** evidence confined to source-supported sauce/gravy thickening. Illinois Extension gives a fixed-liquid comparison of 1 tablespoon cornstarch or 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour per cup of liquid, and describes the resulting appearance difference. This source states both amounts directly; it is not a ratio to infer from another direction. See S1.
- A separate **all-purpose flour → cornstarch** direction is independently supported for sauce/gravy thickening by Illinois Extension and Utah State University Extension: 2 tablespoons flour per cup of liquid corresponds to 1 tablespoon cornstarch, and USU states the inverse direction explicitly as 1 tablespoon flour to 1/2 tablespoon cornstarch. Record it, if accepted, as its own directional finding with a volume basis and fixed liquid context. Do not derive it in the resolver by reversing another finding. See S1–S2.
- The source-supported amounts do not make these methods interchangeable. Flour is commonly cooked with fat as a roux; cornstarch is dispersed in cool liquid and added as a slurry. Cornstarch gives a clearer, glossier sauce; flour gives a cloudier, more opaque sauce. Starch-thickened gravy needs less simmering, can lose body with prolonged boiling, and does not freeze as well. See S1, S3–S4, and S6.
- Keep pudding and pastry-cream uses separate from sauce/gravy. Extension guidance supports flour as a possible thickener in some cooked puddings, but also documents different amounts, preparation, and finish from cornstarch. It does not validate a replacement in the bundled chocolate pudding or strawberry pastry cream formula. Egg-thickened custards are outside this starch substitution unless a given recipe also uses a starch thickener. See S5 and S7.
- No runtime rule is ready. The recipe quantities use inconsistent units and liquid bases, and the active rule model cannot require the thickener role and method. A rule also cannot preserve a switch from roux to slurry, its added dispersing liquid, and its cook-time changes. These are the same integration gaps recorded in the current WP08 review; no shared files were edited for this proposal.

Boundary cases found in the same reference scan:

- `103` Chocolate Syrup, #2, uses 1 1/2 tbsp all-purpose flour with sugar, cocoa, margarine, and milk, then boils for 1 minute. It is a dessert sauce-like product, but the instructions do not explicitly describe thickening or give a target consistency; retain as adjacent, formula-specific demand.
- `104` Asparagus Souffle, #3, uses 2 tbsp all-purpose flour in a butter roux and milk base that is thickened before it is pureed with asparagus, yolks, and cheese, folded with whipped egg whites, and baked. This is a soufflé base/structure use, not a standalone sauce substitution.
- `045` also has 20 g cornstarch with 75 g all-purpose flour in the sponge mixture. Those ingredients are sifted together for cake structure; they are not the pastry-cream thickener pair.
- Other cornstarch rows are `014` blueberry cake filling (2 tbsp), `029` cherry cake filling (2 tbsp cornstarch or arrowroot), `106` blueberry pie filling (1 tbsp), and `108` raspberry pie filling (1 tbsp). These are fruit/pie-filling uses and were not transferred to sauces or custards. `085` Maple Chicken uses 3 tbsp cornstarch to coat chicken before frying, not to thicken its sauce.
- `070` Nanaimo Cake uses 4.5 tbsp custard powder in its filling. It is not linked to cornstarch, and this review does not infer that the powder is pure cornstarch or interchangeable with wheat flour.

The recipe audit therefore finds actual demand for flour-thickened sauces/gravy and cornstarch-thickened pudding/pastry cream, but no ingredient line that labels flour and cornstarch as alternatives. The main flour methods include roux, flour dispersed in milk, and flour added with tomato paste. The cornstarch methods include cold-liquid slurry and tempering with egg yolks. The differing formulas and methods matter more than same-recipe co-occurrence.

## Directional research findings

| Direction and context | Source-supported amount / method | Effects and limits | Disposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cornstarch → all-purpose flour, sauce/gravy thickening | S1 compares 1 tbsp cornstarch per 1 cup liquid with 2 tbsp all-purpose flour per 1 cup liquid. This is a fixed-liquid volume comparison. If flour is made into a roux, source guidance separately pairs it with fat; no universal extra-fat adjustment is stated as part of this swap. | Flour gives a cloudier, more opaque sauce; cornstarch gives a clearer, glossier sauce. S1 says flour is best in sauces made with fat, such as cheese-sauce roux. S3–S4 describe different cooking times and warn that overcooked/held starch-thickened gravy can thin. No exact bundled recipe was tested. | Preserve the existing source-bounded ledger finding. Do not expand to pudding, pie filling, or every gravy. No runtime rule. |
| All-purpose flour → cornstarch, sauce/gravy thickening | S1 gives the same fixed-liquid comparison directly. S2 separately states 1 tbsp flour used as thickener → 1/2 tbsp cornstarch, and 1 tbsp cornstarch → 2 tbsp flour. A slurry method requires dispersing cornstarch in cool liquid before adding it to hot liquid; the reviewed substitution sources do not set a recipe-specific amount of that dispersing liquid or a correction to the recipe's other liquid. | The product shifts from a flour/roux body to a clearer, glossier starch finish. Cornstarch needs less simmering; prolonged boiling can reduce body, and freezing is poor (S3–S4). A sauce that depends on the flour-fat roux's color, flavor, or opaque texture will change. | Separate directional ledger candidate, pending reviewer acceptance of amount basis and method note. This evidence does not justify a generic line-level runtime swap. |
| Cornstarch → flour, cooked pudding/pastry cream | S5 says flour has about half cornstarch's thickening capacity and gives a flour method for pudding: mix flour with lukewarm water, add smoothly, and bring the mixture to a boil. It provides no formula-specific replacement amount or test for recipes `088` or `045`. | Flour needs several minutes of cooking; undercooking can leave starchy flavor and a white appearance. Cornstarch is smoother/glossier and can thin if overcooked or stirred too vigorously (S5). A starch-thickened pudding is distinct from an egg-only custard (S7); some custard recipes do use starch (S5). | Qualitative research candidate only. Do not transfer the sauce/gravy ratio or assert equivalence in the bundled formulas. No runtime rule. |
| All-purpose flour → cornstarch, pudding/pastry cream | No reviewed source gives a tested wheat-flour replacement in the bundled chocolate pudding or Fraisier pastry cream with complete ingredient amounts, dispersing liquid, egg-tempering sequence, and cook time. The general half-capacity statement in S5 is not a formula-specific test. | A change from flour to pure starch affects the amount, slurry, boil/simmer time, appearance, and cooling set. Egg, sugar, cocoa/chocolate, and dairy proportions in the exact recipe may change the outcome. | Unresolved for these recipes. Require a direct test or a closely matched, authoritative recipe comparison before recording a supported substitution. |
| Wheat flour → cornstarch in baked goods, coatings, fruit/pie fillings, custard powder, or non-starch custards | Not supported by this sauce/gravy review. | The corpus includes these adjacent uses, but the source claims above concern thickening liquids. `085` is a coating; `045` sponge and `070` custard powder are different functions/products. | Explicitly exclude; research separately if needed. |

The amount comparisons above are volume measures, not mass conversions. No g-based ratio is supported; neither reference has a density or unit mapping. The reviewed sources also do not establish a single optimal consistency, explain the intended thickness of every bundled recipe, or test the same recipe twice with only the thickener changed.
