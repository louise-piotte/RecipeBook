# WP06 Andean ají amarillo and ají panca boundary review

- **Date:** 2026-10-09
- **Status:** Research proposal only; no canonical catalog changes are made here.
- **Scope:** Assess the identity boundary between Peruvian ají amarillo and ají panca, their fresh and dried forms, ají mirasol naming, and pepper pastes; recommend safe English and fr-CA display names.

## Recommendation

Accept **ají amarillo** and **ají panca** as separate culinary ingredient identities. The reviewed Peruvian sources distinguish their names, fruit appearance, and culinary uses. Treat fresh and dried peppers as states of each named pepper, not as separate pepper identities. In the cited Peruvian sources, ají amarillo when dried is called **ají mirasol**; keep that name scoped to the dried ají amarillo form. Ají panca is commonly marketed dried in one PROMPERÚ product profile, but INIA research documents fresh fruit and use in both fresh and dry states. This market-state difference does not justify a second panca identity. [S01–S05]

Do not attach either pepper's paste name to its raw fruit identity. A Peruvian government recipe lists one fresh ají amarillo alongside separate pasta de ají amarillo and pasta de ají panca ingredients. A Peruvian producer lists differing paste formulations, including salt, and for its amarillo paste onion, oil, and additives. Treat each named paste as a prepared product concept; defer canonical paste identities until catalog scope and formulation are reviewed. Neither a paste nor a ground product should be converted to raw pepper by alias. [S08–S09]

Proposed display names:

| Identity candidate | EN display | fr-CA display | Naming basis and status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ají amarillo | `ají amarillo pepper` | `piment jaune (ají amarillo)` | Retains the source name in both locales. Québec SAQ uses the French phrase `piment jaune (ají amarillo)` in a Peruvian grocery context. The fr-CA wording is provisional; no OQLF terminology entry was identified in the targeted search. [S10] |
| Ají panca | `ají panca pepper` | `piment panca (ají panca)` | Retains the source name to avoid the ambiguous translation “red pepper.” Canadian CIPO trademark goods text uses `piment panca`; this is Canadian French market wording, not a Québec terminology ruling. The fr-CA display is provisional. [S11] |

The species assignments are not sufficiently consistent to encode as hard identity boundaries. INIA and an INIA-affiliated crop study identify ají panca as *Capsicum chinense* and ají amarillo as *C. baccatum*; a PROMPERÚ 2019 export profile lists both as *C. baccatum*. Use the source-named culinary products as identities and do not make a botanical species an alias or an asserted universal taxonomic definition. [S01–S04]

## Product boundary and use evidence

INIA’s accession catalogue records ají amarillo and ají panca separately, with different accession identifiers and mature fruit colors. An INIA-affiliated field study describes ají panca as slightly pungent with reddish-brown mature fruit and fresh/dry use, while ají amarillo is moderately pungent with intensely orange mature fruit and fresh/dry use. Those measurements and sensory descriptors concern the studied accessions and are not universal guarantees. [S01–S02]

PROMPERÚ’s 2015 bilingual publication names ají amarillo and ají escabeche as one product, states that the dried product is ají mirasol, and describes fresh and dry market presentations. The same publication describes panca as a different named pepper, sold dried in the markets it discusses. MIDAGRI independently states that yellow ají becomes ají mirasol when dehydrated. The 2023 Peruvian National Institute of Health food tables also separate fresh amarillo, fresh ground amarillo without salt, dry amarillo, and ají panca entries. These entries support form distinctions, not nutrient transfers or conversion ratios. [S03, S05, S07]

Perú Travel’s recipe for papa a la huancaína names ají amarillo, and its carapulcra recipe names ají panca; those source recipe IDs are already registered as `WP06-SOUTHAM-20261009-S02` and `WP06-SOUTHAM-20261009-S03` and are reused here without duplicate IDs. The Ministry of Production recipe booklet separately lists one fresh ají amarillo, pasta de ají amarillo, and pasta de ají panca. These are recipe-use examples, not standard formulations or evidence that one paste can replace another. [S08; reused SOUTHAM S02–S03]

## Evidence limits

- The strongest product and botanical descriptions are Peruvian. They do not settle naming across every Andean region or every Capsicum accession.
- The documented species disagreement means the proposal accepts the two culinary product names without hard-coding a species. A botanical taxonomy review would need more authoritative cultivar and market evidence.
- PROMPERÚ’s `ají panca sold dry` description concerns the markets described in its publication. INIA’s fresh accession and the 2021 field study prevent that statement from being generalized to all panca fruit or use.
- The government food tables distinguish named presentations, but they are not paste recipes and their nutrient values are not used for app measurements.
- The government recipe booklet proves that recipes distinguish paste inputs from whole fresh pepper; it does not define the commercial paste composition. The manufacturer source is product-specific and shows why a universal paste formula should not be assumed.
- Québec French evidence is limited: SAQ provides a Québec French example for amarillo; CIPO provides Canadian trade-mark goods wording for panca. Neither source is an OQLF terminology decision, so the proposed French displays remain provisional.
- No density, unit mapping, yield, rehydration ratio, heat scale, conversion, or substitution is activated.
