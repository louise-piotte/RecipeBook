# WP05 Section V — beer, cider, and fortified or aromatized wine boundaries

- **Date:** 2026-10-12
- **Status:** proposal only; no catalog, recipe, legacy, ledger, workflow, roadmap, checklist, or plan data changed.
- **Scope:** beer and named beer styles, cooking beer, cider and cider subtypes, sherry, port, Madeira, Marsala, and vermouth.
- **Not re-audited:** plain white/red wine and the salted cooking-wine/mirin product boundaries. Their existing Section V reviews remain the source of truth.
- **Source namespace:** `WP05-BEER-CIDER-FORTIFIED-WINE-20261012-S01` through `S19`.

## Recommendation

Keep beverage identity separate from recipe use. A generic beer or cider ingredient can have an identity of its own; named styles and origin-controlled wines remain distinct when a recipe names them. Do not turn “for cooking” into an alias or a universal product class. The bundled recipes have no ingredient demand for any candidate in this packet, so hold activation, recipe links, and all measurement behavior until a line or imported product provides exact wording.

Use Québec French `bière`, `cidre`, `xérès`, `porto`, `madère`, and `vermouth` as supported below. Keep `Marsala` as a proper regional product name in French and English; the current SAQ French product listing uses that exact label, while the EU specification establishes its PDO scope. Do not equate any of these products with one another or with a broad “fortified wine / vin fortifié” identity.

## Numeric evidence and substitution limits

- The Canadian compositional standard defines cider as alcoholic fermentation of apple juice and sets 2.5–13.0% absolute alcohol by volume. Its separate Champagne-cider standard requires carbonation under pressure and at least 7% absolute alcohol. These regulatory values distinguish product classes; they are not recipe amounts or a cider-to-juice conversion.
- The Canadian beer standard permits at most 4% residual sugars and defines regulatory common-name alcohol ranges for extra-light, light, regular, strong, and extra-strong beer. These are label thresholds, not beer-style conversions or cooking substitutions.
- The CFIA vermouth standard allows no more than 20% absolute alcohol by volume and defines the product by added bitters, aromatics, other botanicals, or flavouring. That does not make vermouth equivalent to plain wine or another fortified wine.
- OQLF's ice-cider entry reports its product-standard criteria and explicitly distinguishes it from generic cider. The SAQ's current Québec example is an IGP product with a product-specific label and ABV; neither establishes a kitchen measure.
- No reviewed source supports ingredient density, a beer/cider/wine volume-to-mass conversion, a cooking reduction factor, a universal alcohol-retention value, or a substitution among beer, cider, sherry, port, Madeira, Marsala, and vermouth. Add no measures, density, conversions, yields, package quantities, or substitution rules.
