# WP02 Other Roasted Gram Flour Product-Boundary Review

- **Date:** 2026-10-11
- **Status:** Root reviewed and integrated the unresolved decision. Five source entries were added to the existing candidate ledger record; no active reference, alias, recipe, form, measurement, conversion, yield, or substitution changed.
- **Scope:** Recheck other roasted Bengal gram/chickpea products, especially *bhuna chana*, *pottukadalai*, and *pottukadalai maavu*, against besan, generic chickpea flour, the existing chana-sattu identity, and Niru's roasted urid/Mong products. This is a pulse-product boundary review, separate from the recent root-flour terminology review.
- **Source IDs:** `WP02-ROASTED-GRAM-PRODUCT-BOUNDARY-20261011-S01` through `S12`. S01, S02, S05, S06, and S07 are registered on `candidate-pottukadalai-maavu`; the other IDs remain proposal evidence.

## Recommendation

Keep `candidate-pottukadalai-maavu` unresolved. Do not create a new active flour identity or add `pottukadalai`, `pottukadalai maavu`, `bhuna chana`, or `roasted gram flour` as aliases.

The sources support several product contexts, but do not establish a separate, stable Canadian/Québec flour class for catalog allocation:

- Canadian retailer Ameen Foods sells a whole, unsalted roasted chickpea product, identifies its ingredient as *Cicer arietinum*, calls the product `bhuna chana` and `pottukadalai` among a broad synonym list, and suggests grinding it into coarse roasted gram flour. This is a whole-chickpea product page with a suggested home use, not a flour product specification or French flour label [S05].
- An Indian roasted-chana-dal manufacturer identifies its product as split Bengal gram, dry-roasted, and calls that product `Pottukadalai` and `Bhuna Chana Dal`. It says the dal can be ground into powders, but does not define a flour grade [S06]. A South Indian recipe source uses `pottukadalai maavu` for ground fried Bengal gram and lists it separately from Bengal gram flour [S07]. Together these sources support a regional roasted-dal context; they do not settle whether every product called pottukadalai flour is distinct from chana sattu.
- FSSAI defines chana sattu as flour from roasted *Cicer arietinum* grains after grinding. Its published definition does not set a split/dehusked boundary that would distinguish every pottukadalai-flour use from sattu [S01]. Canadian product naming supports the existing chana-sattu identity and its current aliases [S03–S04].
- FSSAI defines besan as ground dehusked Bengal gram. eNAM separately lists chickpea flour (besan) and roasted Bengal gram flour (chana sattu), supporting the existing preparation boundary [S01–S02].

Retain `ingredient-ref-chana-sattu` as the source-defined roasted Bengal gram flour identity. Keep its current aliases `roasted Bengal gram flour`, `roasted chickpea flour`, and `farine de pois chiches grillés`; these already have product/standard support. Leave the separate generic `ingredient-ref-chickpea-flour` unchanged. Do not turn the broad term `roasted gram flour` into a global alias: the English phrase covers regional pulse uses, while the current evidence does not tie it to one unambiguous catalog product.

Keep Niru's `roasted urid flour` identity separate: its Canadian manufacturer says it mills urid flour first and then slow-roasts it. Keep Niru's `Roasted Mong Flour` candidate unresolved: its product page calls the item a “very fine grain starch” from “mong beans” without botanical species or a clear flour/starch or treatment-order definition [S10–S12]. The current Canadian Food Inspection Agency and OQLF wording distinguishes *Vigna mungo* / haricot urd from *Vigna radiata*, while OQLF warns that French `haricot mungo` can denote both species [S09]. No roasted-gram alias should bridge these pulse products.

No new French name is proposed. Québec sources support `farine de pois chiche` for generic chickpea flour, and a Canadian French Sattu product listing uses `farine de pois chiches grillés`; neither source provides a Québec French product label for `pottukadalai maavu` [S04, S08]. French whole-chickpea snack labels use terms such as `pois chiches rôtis` and `pois chiches grillés`; those product labels do not establish a flour name [S08].

### Why the whole-roast names do not resolve the flour candidate

The current Canadian Ameen Foods listing calls its product `Roasted Unsalted Chickpeas (Nakhod)`, lists only chickpeas and *Cicer arietinum*, and says the product can be ground into coarse roasted gram flour. Its “also known as” list includes `bhuna chana`, `pottukadalai`, `Bengal gram`, `kabuli chana`, and other terms. That is useful Canadian-market evidence for this one whole-chickpea SKU, but the long alias list does not define those terms as universal synonyms or show a commercially packaged flour [S05].

The product-specific Indian source gives a narrower split-dal context for Pottukadalai: selected Bengal gram is split and dry-roasted. A regional recipe source then describes grinding fried Bengal gram into `pottukadalai maavu` [S06–S07]. The FSSAI chana-sattu definition, however, says roasted Bengal gram grains are ground without specifying a split/dehusked state. The reviewed evidence therefore cannot prove either complete synonymy or a consistent flour distinction between pottukadalai maavu and chana sattu. A separate identity or alias would encode more than these sources establish.

## Evidence limits

- FSSAI's chana-sattu definition establishes roasted *Cicer arietinum* grains followed by grinding, but it does not specify the hull/split condition needed to distinguish all South Indian roasted-dal flour contexts.
- Ameen Foods is a current Canadian seller shipping to Ontario and Québec. Its page is primary evidence for the wording and composition of its own whole roasted chickpea SKU; it is not a flour specification, and its synonym list is too broad to support global aliases.
- Organic Mandya is a manufacturer source for a specific Indian roasted split-Bengal-gram product. It documents regional names and processing, not a Canadian bilingual label or a standardized flour class.
- The pottukadalai-maavu recipe source is useful for its recipe-specific ingredient wording and home grinding sequence; it is not a regulatory definition or retail package.
- Spice Divine's Canadian French Sattu page was visible at access but marked out of stock. It supports the bilingual product wording shown there, not current availability or a Québec terminology ruling.
- Québec whole-chickpea snack labels support `pois chiches rôtis` / `pois chiches grillés` for those whole snack products. They do not support a compound flour label for pottukadalai maavu.
- No current Québec French product label or OQLF entry for `pottukadalai maavu` was located in this bounded search. No French translation is proposed.

## Integration recommendation

1. Keep the existing chana-sattu and generic chickpea-flour identities and aliases unchanged.
2. Keep `candidate-pottukadalai-maavu` unresolved and `candidate-roasted-mong-flour` unresolved. Do not add active rows, aliases, recipe links, forms, measurements, density, unit mappings, conversions, yields, or substitutions.
3. Do not assign `bhuna chana`, `pottukadalai`, `pottukadalai maavu`, `roasted gram flour`, `gram flour`, or `besan` to a global identity. Resolve a future recipe only from its named product, pulse state, and source context.
4. Reopen the flour decision if a current Canadian bilingual package or manufacturer specification identifies the pottukadalai flour's grain state and process, supplies a stable product name, and provides Québec French wording. Keep any new identity product-specific rather than promoting the broad roasted-gram phrase.
