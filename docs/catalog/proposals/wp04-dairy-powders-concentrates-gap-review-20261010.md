# WP04 proposal: Dairy powders, milk solids, and concentrates

- **Research date:** 2026-10-10
- **Status:** narrow identity and form decisions integrated; see `docs/catalog/batches/wp04-dairy-powders-concentrates-identity-integration-20261010/README.md`.
- **Scope:** Section Q powder and concentrate gap, related live dairy identities, exact bundled recipe demand, English/Québec French naming, and product-composition boundaries.
- **Boundary:** source IDs in this proposal are local. No values, aliases, forms, or substitutions have been added to the shared package.

## Recommendation

The Section Q gap is real: the active package has no milk-powder, whey-powder, buttermilk-powder, milk-solids, or malted-milk-powder reference or form. Canadian standards and OQLF terminology support distinct product names for whole milk powder, skim milk powder, buttermilk powder, whey powder, acid-type whey powder, and malted milk powder. Preserve these as distinct product concepts when the plan is integrated. Do not create a generic `milk solids` identity or a broad “dairy concentrate” identity.

There is **no direct powder or milk-solids demand in the current 114-recipe bundle**. It contains four concentrate lines: three sweetened-condensed-milk lines and one evaporated-milk line. It also contains six liquid buttermilk lines, which do not establish demand for a dry powder. Identity-only catalog coverage can still be added from the standards below, but the recipe corpus supplies no grounds for recipe links, density, kitchen measures, reconstitution ratios, or substitution rules.

For future catalog modeling, use a powder form only where the active parent reference is a verified match for the source product: whole-milk powder under the whole-milk reference, and skim-milk powder under the separate skim-milk reference. Do not make buttermilk powder a form of the current cultured-buttermilk recipe identity without a source defining that product relationship. Keep whey powder and malted milk powder as separate product concepts; malted milk powder is a compound product. Hold partially skimmed, sweet/acid whey, protein-concentrate, blended-powder, and specialty process variants until an exact source or recipe calls for them.

### Composition boundaries (do not store as density or recipe conversions)

- Canadian standards define whole milk powder as dried milk containing at least 95% milk solids and 26% milk fat. Skim milk powder is dried skim milk containing at least 95% milk solids. The separate dry-product grading tables give grade-specific fat limits for spray-dried skim powder; those grade limits are not a universal product-level fat value.
- The Canadian Grade Compendium differentiates spray-dried buttermilk powder and whey powder by their own grade requirements. It separates acid-type whey powder from whey powder and sets a higher minimum titratable acidity for the acid-type grade. These grade numbers do not yield a kitchen measure, density, or substitution ratio.
- The federal malted-milk-powder standard requires milk combined with the liquid from a ground-barley-malt-and-meal mash; it specifies at least 7.5% milk fat and at most 3.5% moisture. This establishes a compound-product boundary, not a universal formula for every branded malted milk powder.
- The federal standard defines sweetened condensed milk with added sweetening ingredients and minimum milk-solids/fat requirements (28% and 8%). Evaporated milk is separately defined as water-removed milk with minimum 25% milk solids and 7.5% milk fat. Evaporated skim and evaporated partly skimmed/concentrated partly skimmed products have separate standards. Do not merge them or treat “concentrate” as a single product.
- The federal “milk solids” ingredient definition excludes water and, for non-cheese dairy products, casein; it differs from a consumer product identity. No line in the bundle specifies a milk-solids powder or solids percentage.

These composition statements establish product boundaries only. They do not supply a default mass-per-cup, density, scoop weight, yield, or amount of powder needed to reconstitute milk.

## Alias, measurement, and substitution limits

- Keep `whole milk powder` and `skim milk powder` separate. Do not attach unqualified `milk powder`, `powdered milk`, or `lait en poudre` to either product without source-specific fat-class evidence. If the seed uses forms, put exact product names and aliases on the matching whole- or skim-milk form.
- The safe language pairs and their narrow spelling variants are listed in the product table. Do not add `whey` as an alias for whey powder, `milk solids` as a powder alias, or `malted milk powder` as a malt-extract/flour alias.
- No source reviewed gives a cup, tablespoon, or teaspoon mass for the intended generic powders. Canadian grading materials report packaging-size examples and product composition, not volumetric kitchen measures. Do not add densities or unit mappings from them.
- No reconstitution ratio was carried forward: sources vary by product class, fat level, grade, heat treatment, and intended use, and the recipe bundle has no powder line needing one. In particular, do not derive a universal milk-powder-to-water or buttermilk-powder-to-liquid ratio.
- No powder substitution is proposed. The existing WP08 cultured-buttermilk/acidified-milk work is a liquid, recipe-context finding; the sour-cream/Greek-yogurt findings concern cultured wet products. Neither justifies powder-to-liquid or powder-to-powder equivalence. The active substitution arrays remain empty; the old cooking-cream-to-evaporated-milk ledger record remains blocked.

## Québec French review of existing concentrate labels

This powder audit also found a label issue on the two already-existing concentrate references:

- OQLF entries prefer `lait concentré` over `lait évaporé`, describing the latter as an improper or discouraged term. Current CFIA French identity language also uses `lait évaporé ou lait concentré`, and the bundled Sally’s source line is in English. Because `lait concentré` is also a broader family phrase that can include skim/partly skimmed and sweetened products in general explanations, do not add it as a shared alias without a bounded reviewer decision. Consider a product-qualified display such as `lait concentré non sucré`; keep the source wording on `114#17` intact.
- OQLF prefers `lait concentré sucré` and marks `lait condensé` as discouraged. Current CFIA French standard wording and the Eagle Brand Québec recipe page use `lait condensé sucré`. This is a preferred-display versus source-label tension on the existing `ingredient-ref-sweetened-condensed-milk`, not a new product or alias row. Consider changing only the catalog display to `lait concentré sucré` and retaining the source-label phrase as a search alias if the localization reviewer accepts it.
- Do not merge the products: the sweetened standard adds sugar/dextrose/glucose/lactose and has different minimum composition requirements from unsweetened evaporated/concentrated milk.

These wording observations do not change the source-specific quantities in `026#3` or `114#17`, and they do not authorize a can-size rule.
