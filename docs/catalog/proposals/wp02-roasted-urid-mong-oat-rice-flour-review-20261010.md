## Product and terminology boundaries

### Roasted urid flour

Niru's current product page is titled “Urid Flour (Roasted)” and its description says the lentils are finely milled and then slow roasted. The manufacturer's current Rice & Flour page lists this item separately from “Urid Flour,” supporting a distinct roasted flour grade rather than a synonym for urid grain or ordinary urid flour [S01, S05]. CFIA's French table identifies Vigna mungo as haricot urd and separately identifies Vigna radiata as haricot mungo; OQLF notes that French haricot mungo is used for both species, while its English mapping for Vigna mungo includes urd and black gram [S02–S03].

Recommend the exact English product class roasted urid flour, not an open-ended roasted-gram class. The proposed French display farine de haricot urd torréfiée retains the CFIA species discriminator. Avoid bare farine de haricot mungo because it can point to either Vigna mungo or Vigna radiata. The Québec mill's published roasted-flour wording supports torréfiée as a local process adjective, but it does not attest the full urid phrase [S13]. Do not add urad, urd, black gram, lentil flour, or plain urid flour as aliases without product-specific evidence. Do not record a roast temperature, duration, or any behavior beyond Niru's “slow roasted” wording.

### Roasted mong flour

Niru has a current product page and listing under the exact name “Roasted Mong Flour.” Its own description calls the item a “very fine grain starch” made from “mong beans,” without botanical identity, extraction details, or an explicit roast/mill sequence [S04–S06]. The product list also distinguishes “Roasted Mong” from “Roasted Mong Flour,” so the grain product must not be collapsed into its flour-named counterpart [S06–S07].

Keep it held. The catalog already has separate mung-bean starch, mung-bean grain, and bean-thread-noodle identities. The page does not resolve whether this product is bean flour, extracted starch, or a named regional flour product; neither the word mong nor the word flour settles that boundary. Do not map it to ingredient-ref-mung-bean-starch, the mung-bean ingredient, chana sattu, or an assumed Vigna species. Ask the manufacturer for the bean's botanical name and whether the item is milled whole bean or extracted starch, with the treatment order and a French product name.

### Red and white rice products

Niru currently lists “Roasted Red Rice Flour”; its product page says it is flour made from finely milled red rice and gives package sizes and culinary uses. That exact, current manufacturer-named product is sufficient for the narrow market identity roasted red rice flour, even though the page does not state whether rice is roasted before milling or flour after milling [S05, S07]. The candidate remains separate from whole red rice and the existing generic, white, and brown rice-flour references. Do not add red rice flour, roasted rice flour, or a technical process description as an alias or property.

The proposed French display farine de riz rouge torréfiée combines OQLF's generic farine de riz with the Canadian manufacturer's exact red-rice product qualifier and the Québec mill's published use of torréfié for named heat-treated flours [S08, S12–S13]. Neither Niru nor OQLF directly attests the complete French product phrase. Treat that wording as provisional and leave independent Québec review open; do not add it as a French alias.

Niru's older product-list PDF names roasted white rice flour with package SKUs, but the current Rice & Flour page lists roasted red rice flour and not the white product [S05, S07]. Hold the white-rice candidate until a current product page or current bilingual package confirms that it remains a marketed flour product. The generic ingredient-ref-white-rice-flour remains distinct and unchanged.

### Oat product

The live English product page and French counterpart title the item “Pregelatinized Oat Flour” / “Farine d'Avoine Prégélatinisé.” Their descriptions characterize the functional range as heat-treated and discuss roasting; the same body text then calls the item “Roasted Oat Flour” / “Farine d'Avoine Torréfiée.” No page supplies a clear product specification that establishes pregelatinization, says roasting is the sole process, or explains whether the two terms denote the same flour [S09–S10].

Hold both candidate names and do not add either as an alias of the other or of generic oat flour. OQLF defines farine d'avoine as flour obtained by milling oat grains, whole or not; that supports the base Québec French term only, not either processing grade [S11]. Ask the mill to confirm its marketed product name, whether the flour is pregelatinized, the order of milling and thermal treatment, and the preferred French product name. Do not transfer the page's bakery-performance, nutritional, shelf-life, or health claims into the catalog.
