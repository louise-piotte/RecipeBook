## Product boundaries

### Hattai-ko and mugikogashi

Japan's current food-composition database classifies `麦こがし` as **CEREALS / Barley / roasted flour** and gives *Hordeum vulgare*. Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (MAFF) describes Hattai-ko / mugikogashi as roasted barley flour in the Oita Yaseuma dish, where it may be used instead of kinako. The Yaseuma page is one regional recipe context; it does not define a general Hattai-ko substitution [S01–S02].

Maehara Seifun's product overview says the cereal input varies: it describes barley in eastern Japan, naked barley in western Japan, and corn or millet in some localities. Its current 120 g product is specifically headed Hattai-ko / mugikogashi and states 100% domestic barley. The exact SKU therefore supports a barley product, while the family names alone do not identify one cereal everywhere [S03–S04]. The maker's English rendering of its regional description uses “rye” for 裸麦; this review follows the Japanese source's naked-barley wording rather than treating the translation as a separate rye product.

Keep the broad names off `ingredient-ref-roasted-barley-flour`. The current Québec row is a named functional flour with a developed heat-treatment process; its public description does not establish that it shares the Japanese product's grain-roasting-then-milling sequence or performance. OQLF supports the base pair `barley flour / farine d'orge`, and the Québec mill uses `farine d'orge torréfiée` for its own product. That supports the existing localized name, but not a Japanese product equivalence [S05–S06]. A recipe that names Hattai-ko and separately confirms barley may be manually resolved to the closest current barley-flour identity; the name by itself should stay unmatched.

Do not use the MAFF Yaseuma note to add a global Kinako substitution. It says Hattai-ko can be used in place of kinako in that dish; it does not establish a ratio, general use scope, or equivalence between roasted barley and roasted soy flour.

### Roasted Bengal gram, besan, and chana sattu

The FSSAI standard defines besan as flour from ground, dehusked Bengal gram (*Cicer arietinum*). Separately, it defines roasted Bengal gram flour (Chana Sattu) as ground roasted gram. The Chana Sattu provision does not say the gram must be split or dehusked, so it does not establish a precise split-state boundary against every regional flour made from roasted chana dal [S07]. The official National Agriculture Market's commodity material also lists chickpea flour (Besan) and roasted Bengal gram flour (Chana Sattu) separately [S08].

The live catalog already reflects the supported distinction:

- `ingredient-ref-chickpea-flour` is `chickpea flour / farine de pois chiche`; its only alias is the plural French `farine de pois chiches`.
- `ingredient-ref-chana-sattu` is `chana sattu / farine de sattu`; it owns the source-backed roasted Bengal gram/chickpea flour aliases listed above.
- The Canadian French-market 24 Mantra Sattu page calls its product `farine de pois chiches grillés`. The listing is out of stock, so it supports the bilingual product wording, not current availability or a terminology-authority ruling [S09].

Retain these rows and aliases exactly. Do not promote bare `roasted gram flour`, `gram flour`, `besan`, or `sattu` into aliases. These terms may refer to different pulses, husking states, grain blends, or roast states across sources. The current chana-sattu alias `roasted chickpea flour` is supported for that named product and should not be generalized to every roasted chickpea flour.

### Pottukadalai maavu and the live roasted-chana-dal form

The official Roasted Bengal Gram (Split) grading rules define a roasted split Bengal-gram grain product. A current South Indian manufacturer calls its ready-to-eat split, dry-roasted chana dal Pottukadalai and lists regional names including Bhuna Chana Dal. It says that this grain can be ground into powders. A regional recipe source uses `pottukadalai maavu` and lists the resulting flour separately from besan. These sources support real regional wording and a grain-to-powder use, but they do not define a stable flour specification or settle its exact boundary from Chana Sattu [S10–S12].

The **current live package has changed since some earlier phrase audits**: `ingredient-form-desi-chickpeas-roasted-split-dehulled` is now present under `ingredient-ref-desi-chickpeas`. Its English display is `roasted split Bengal gram`, with match terms `roasted Bengal gram split`, `roasted gram split`, `roasted chana split`, `roasted chana dal`, and `roasted split chickpeas`. Its French display `pois chiches Desi cassés et rôtis` is documented in the form notes as descriptive, with no exact Canadian product name or French match terms. The form notes explicitly distinguish the pulse form from flour. Thus, exact search for `roasted chana dal` currently has a **form owner**, not a flour-reference owner.

Keep `candidate-pottukadalai-maavu` unresolved. Do not map the flour to the grain form, generic chickpea flour, or Chana Sattu. The sources leave open whether the flour's split/dehulled state is a catalog-relevant product distinction or simply one way of making roasted gram flour. A current manufacturer specification and a Québec/Canadian French product name would be needed before adding another identity or alias.

Whole roasted chickpea pages also use broad names such as `bhuna chana` and `pottukadalai`, sometimes with a suggestion to grind the snack at home. Such whole-pulse listings do not establish a packaged flour identity. Keep those product names source-scoped, especially where a page's synonym list is broader than the product's stated composition.

### Urid and Mong products

Niru's current Canadian pages distinguish plain `Urid Flour` from `Urid Flour (Roasted)` and describe the latter as flour milled first and then slow-roasted. The current catalog's `roasted urid flour / farine de haricot urd torréfiée` row is therefore appropriately separate from plain urid flour and from the Cicer-based products [S15].

Niru's current `Roasted Mong Flour` page instead calls it a “very fine grain starch” made from “mong beans.” It provides no botanical species, extraction method, or order of roasting and milling. The current Rice & Flour collection lists this product separately from `Roasted Mung`, but that separate listing still does not resolve what the flour product contains. Keep `candidate-roasted-mong-flour` unresolved and distinct from the active `mung bean starch` reference until the maker confirms species and whether it is milled whole pulse or extracted starch [S13–S14].

## Reopen criteria

- **Hattai-ko / mugikogashi:** a source identifying the grain for the exact product or recipe and Canadian/Québec evidence for its French product display. Any alias must be scoped to a stable barley-only class; do not use the family names alone.
- **Pottukadalai maavu:** a current manufacturer/package specification for the flour, including the pulse species and split/hull state, enough process detail to distinguish it from the already active chana-sattu row, plus Québec/Canadian French wording.
- **Roasted Mong Flour:** maker confirmation of botanical species, whole-pulse flour versus extracted starch, roast/mill order, and a usable French product name.
